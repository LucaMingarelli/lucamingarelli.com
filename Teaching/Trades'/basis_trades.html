The basis trade

Two prices for the same asset should agree by delivery day. When they don't, the gap between them — the basis — is either free money or a very patient way to lose your shirt. Here is the arithmetic, and the risk hiding inside it.

futures price F(t) spot price S(t) basis B(t) = F(t) − S(t)
t = today → T = delivery 365 days to expiry

§1

Defining the basis

For any asset with a futures contract — a Treasury note, a barrel of oil, an equity index — two prices exist at once: the spot price you would pay to own it right now, and the futures price you would lock in today for delivery later. The basis is simply the difference.

Definition 1 — Basis

At the instant a futures contract expires, it converges to spot — delivery forces it to. So the basis is a number engineered to hit exactly zero on a known date. That is what makes basis trades feel like arbitrage: you are not betting on where the market goes, you are betting on a difference collapsing to a value you already know.

§2

Why the basis isn't zero today

If you could buy the asset now and hold it for free, spot and futures would already be equal. They aren't, because holding costs something (financing) and sometimes pays something back (dividends, coupons, convenience yield). This is the cost-of-carry model.

Definition 2 — Cost of carry
r = financing rate · q = yield earned while holding the asset · T−t = time to delivery

Rearranged, the "fair" basis is just the net cost of carrying the asset to delivery.

Corollary 2.1 — Fair basis

When the market's basis drifts away from this fair value, a mechanical trade appears: buy the cheap side, sell the expensive side, hold to delivery, collect the difference.

§3

The trade itself

The classic version is cash-and-carry: the basis is "too wide," so you buy the underlying and sell the future against it, financing the purchase until delivery.

Long leg — buy spot

Borrow cash at rate r, buy the underlying asset today at S₀. You now own it and owe the loan.

t=0: −S₀ (buy) · +S₀ (borrow)
t=T: repay S₀e^{rT}, deliver the asset
Short leg — sell the future

Sell a futures contract at F₀, obligating you to deliver the asset at T for a price fixed today.

t=0: no cash (futures are margined)
t=T: receive F₀ on delivery

Net it out and the profit is locked in at inception, regardless of where the market goes in between.

Proposition 3 — Locked-in profit

That is the textbook version. In practice you do not hold to delivery in peace — you hold through a path, and the path is where the risk actually lives. That is what the simulator below is for.

Algorithm 4.2 — Discretisation

But basis widening is not the only way you get forced out. The two legs of the trade are financed completely differently, and that matters.

Long bond — financed in repo

The bond secures its own financing: you repo it out, pledging the bond itself as collateral for the cash that bought it. You only need equity to cover the haircut — typically 1–3% of the bond's value.

Short future — margined in cash

The clearinghouse marks this leg to market daily and demands cash variation margin as the basis moves against you. This is the channel modelled by the margin-call threshold below.

The repo leg introduces a second, independent failure mode: the lender can raise the haircut or refuse to roll the loan at all — a funding shock — forcing you to post more equity or unwind, even if the basis itself hasn't moved. Model it as a Poisson-arriving shock with hazard rate h (expected shocks per year).

Model 4.3 — Funding shocks
h = funding-stress rate you set below · a shock forces an immediate unwind at whatever the basis happens to be that day, independent of the margin-call channel

§6

When the "arbitrage" breaks

Cash-and-carry only looks riskless if you can fund the position all the way to delivery. Two episodes show what happens when that assumption fails.

  • 1998

    Long-Term Capital Management ran highly levered basis and relative-value trades across bond markets. When Russia defaulted and spreads widened instead of converging, margin calls forced the fund to unwind at the worst possible moment — turning positions that were "right" on paper into realised losses.

  • 2020

    Hedge funds running the Treasury cash-futures basis trade at high leverage were caught when the March COVID shock widened the basis sharply. Forced deleveraging amplified the sell-off in a market usually considered among the safest in the world.

The lesson isn't that the math is wrong — convergence at delivery is close to certain. It's that leverage turns a small, temporary, adverse move into a forced exit, and forced exits happen before the "sure thing" gets to pay off.
