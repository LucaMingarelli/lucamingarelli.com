Yield Curve Arbitrage

A government bond is not a bet on interest rates. It is a bet on one point of a curve relative to its neighbours — and essentially all of the difficulty in this trade lives in the words “relative to”.

fitted curve · observed bonds · residual = rich / cheap largest residual 0.0 bp at 7y
fitted curve \(\hat y(\tau)\) cheap (yield above fit) rich (yield below fit)

One object, many prices

At any instant a sovereign issuer has dozens or hundreds of bonds outstanding, each with its own coupon, maturity and liquidity. They are not independent securities: they are all claims on the same discount function, and the entire market can in principle be summarised by a single curve.

Definition 1 — Discount function and zero curve
\begin{equation} P_j=\sum_{k} c_{jk}\,D(t_k),\qquad D(\tau)=e^{-y(\tau)\,\tau}, \end{equation} with \(P_j\) the dirty price of bond \(j\), \(c_{jk}\) its cash flows and \(y(\tau)\) the continuously-compounded zero rate.
every bond is a portfolio of the same primitives — the curve is over-determined by the market

Because there are far more bonds than there are degrees of freedom in a smooth curve, the system is over-determined: no smooth \(D(\tau)\) prices every bond exactly. The mismatch is not an error to be minimised away. It is the raw material of the trade.

Where the risk actually lives

Before asking what is cheap, ask what moves. Apply a principal-component decomposition to daily changes in zero rates across maturities and the answer is famously concentrated: three components account for nearly all of the variance, and they have stable, interpretable shapes.

Definition 2 — Factor representation
\begin{equation} \Delta y(\tau_i,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ell_k(\tau_i)\,\Delta F_k(t)+\Delta u_i(t), \qquad K=3 , \end{equation} with \(\ell_1\approx\) const (level), \(\ell_2\) monotone in \(\tau\) (slope), \(\ell_3\) humped (curvature).
\(u_i\) = the idiosyncratic residual of bond \(i\) — the only part a curve arbitrageur wants to own

Those percentages are indicative, not constants of nature: they are estimated from a particular market over a particular window and they move around considerably — in a policy-driven regime the level share rises, at the zero lower bound the slope share does. Litterman and Scheinkman (1991) is the canonical reference; re-run it on your own data before quoting a number.

The strategic point is a ratio. The residual \(u_i\) is worth a handful of basis points and mean-reverts over weeks. The level factor moves that much before lunch. Unhedged, the signal is invisible beneath the noise, and the trade is not a trade at all — it is a rates position with a story attached.

Fitting fair value

“Cheap” only means anything relative to a fitted curve, so the fitting method is part of the strategy, not a preliminary to it. Two families dominate: parsimonious parametric forms, and splines.

Definition 3 — Nelson–Siegel(–Svensson)
\begin{equation} \hat y(\tau)=\beta_0+\beta_1\frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}}{\lambda\tau} +\beta_2\!\left(\frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}}{\lambda\tau}-e^{-\lambda\tau}\right) \;(+\;\beta_3\,\text{second hump}) . \end{equation}
the three loadings are level, slope and curvature — the factor model and the fitting model are the same object
Definition 4 — Rich / cheap residual
\begin{equation} u_i = y_i - \hat y(\tau_i),\qquad z_i = \frac{u_i}{\sigma_{\rm eq}},\qquad \sigma_{\rm eq}=\frac{\sigma_u}{\sqrt{2\kappa_u}} . \end{equation} \(u_i>0\): the bond yields more than the curve says it should — its price is low — it is cheap.
Parametric — few parameters, strong prior

Nelson–Siegel cannot bend enough to chase individual bonds, so residuals are large and persistent. Good: they are stable. Bad: a genuine structural kink in the curve is reported as a whole cluster of “mispriced” bonds.

Spline — many parameters, weak prior

A cubic or B-spline with enough knots will fit almost anything, which means residuals are small and unstable. Move a knot and a bond flips from rich to cheap. Roughness penalties (Fisher–Nychka–Zervos, Waggoner) exist precisely to arbitrate this.

This is the structural difference from a pairs trade. There, the hedge ratio \(\beta\) is one estimated number and you can worry about it directly. Here the entire notion of fair value is a modelling choice, and two desks looking at the same prices with different knot placements will disagree about the sign of the signal. Any curve arbitrageur who has not tested how their residuals behave under a change of fitting method does not know what they are trading.

Hedging the factors away

Sensitivity is measured in DV01: the money change in value for a one-basis-point yield move. A position is a vector of DV01s \(q\) across bonds, and its exposure to factor \(k\) is \(a_k=\sum_i q_i\,\ell_k(\tau_i)\).

Definition 5 — Factor-neutral hedge
Hold one unit of DV01 in the target bond \(i_0\) and choose hedge DV01s \(h_j\), \(j=1,\dots,n\), solving \begin{equation} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \ell_k(\tau_j)\,h_j = -\,\ell_k(\tau_{i_0}), \qquad k=1,\dots,n . \end{equation}
square system: \(n\) hedge instruments neutralise exactly \(n\) factors

With \(n=1\) this is the familiar DV01-neutral pair. With \(n=2\) it is a butterfly: body against two wings, neutral to level and slope. With \(n=3\) you add a fourth instrument and neutralise curvature as well, which is what most systematic curve books actually run.

Proposition 6 — What is left
\begin{equation} \mathrm d\Pi = -\Big[\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k>n} a_k\,\mathrm dF_k}_{\text{unhedged factors}} \;+\;\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_i q_i\,\mathrm du_i}_{\text{residual basket}}\Big] \;+\;\underbrace{\theta\,\mathrm dt}_{\text{carry \& roll}} . \end{equation}

Read the second term carefully, because it is the one people forget. The hedges have residuals too. Neutralising the level factor by shorting a 10y does not give you a clean bet on the 7y's cheapness; it gives you a bet on the 7y's cheapness minus the 10y's. Hedging removes factor risk and adds idiosyncratic risk, and there is a floor below which no amount of further hedging helps.

DV01-neutral is not risk-neutral. Three things break the equivalence: convexity (a large parallel move makes money for the longer-duration leg regardless of direction, so a DV01-neutral barbell is not P&L-neutral); non-parallel moves within a factor, which is exactly what \(u\) is; and the fact that \(\ell_k\) are estimated loadings whose sampling error propagates straight into \(h\).

The trade, leg by leg

Long the cheap bond — financed in repo

Buy it, repo it out, fund only the haircut. Cheap bonds are usually cheap for a reason: off-the-run, poorly indexed, awkward size. They finance at or near general collateral, which is the good news.

earn coupon · pay GC repo · roll down the curve
Short the rich bonds — borrowed in reverse repo

To short you must borrow the bond. Rich bonds are frequently rich because everyone wants them: on-the-run issues trade special, and you lend cash below GC to get them. That spread is a daily bleed, and it can widen without warning.

pay coupon · earn special repo < GC · fail risk

The financing asymmetry is the mirror image of the Treasury basis trade: there the risk was losing the repo on your long, here it is the cost of borrowing your short. And note the uncomfortable structural fact — the specialness that makes a bond rich is also the carry cost of being short it. A large part of the apparent mispricing is a fee, not a free lunch, which is why net carry has to be modelled explicitly rather than assumed to be zero.

A small stochastic model

Four bonds — 2y, 7y, 10y, 30y — on a Nelson–Siegel curve with three mean-reverting factors and one idiosyncratic residual per bond, all in basis points:

\begin{align} \mathrm dF_k &= -\kappa_F F_k\,\mathrm dt+\sigma_k\,\mathrm dW_k, \qquad k=1,2,3,\\ \mathrm du_i &= -\kappa_u u_i\,\mathrm dt+\sigma_u\,\mathrm dZ_i, \qquad i=1,\dots,4,\\ y_i(t) &= \bar y(\tau_i)+\textstyle\sum_k \ell_k(\tau_i)F_k(t)+u_i(t). \end{align}

The target is the 7y; hedges are drawn in the order 10y, 2y, 30y as \(n\) increases. Because the position is a fixed vector of DV01s, the whole P&L collapses to a single scalar path

\begin{equation} G_t=\sum_{k} a_k F_k(t)+\sum_i q_i u_i(t), \qquad \Pi = s\,(G_{t_{\rm in}}-G_t)+s\,\theta\,(t-t_{\rm in})-c , \end{equation}

with \(s=\pm1\) the direction. Everything is linear in the \(\sigma\)s, so the sliders rescale a fixed noise realisation rather than redrawing it, exactly as before.

Two frictions are made explicit. The hedge error \(\epsilon\) multiplies every hedge DV01 by \((1+\epsilon)\), so factor exposure leaks back in proportionally — this stands in for loading estimation error, stale betas and integer-lot rounding. And leverage: equity is set at \(100/L\) basis points of the target leg's DV01, so the book is wiped out if cumulative P&L breaches \(-100/L\) bp. With \(L=10\) you survive a 10 bp adverse move on the hedged spread, and no more. 

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

def ns_loadings(tau, lam=0.6):
    x = lam * np.asarray(tau, float)
    a = (1 - np.exp(-x)) / x
    return np.vstack([np.ones_like(x), a, a - np.exp(-x)]).T   # (n_tau, 3)

# --- fair value: fit NS to observed zero yields, residual = rich/cheap -------
L      = ns_loadings(mats)
beta,*_= np.linalg.lstsq(L, y_obs, rcond=None)
resid  = y_obs - L @ beta                     # in bp;  >0 = cheap

# --- hedge: neutralise the first n factors ----------------------------------
def hedge(i0, hedge_idx, n, lam=0.6):
    """DV01 weights hedging factors 1..n for a unit DV01 in bond i0."""
    A = ns_loadings(mats[hedge_idx], lam)[:, :n].T      # (n, n)
    b = -ns_loadings([mats[i0]], lam)[0, :n]            # (n,)
    return np.linalg.solve(A, b)

# --- sanity: the empirical loadings, not the assumed ones -------------------
dy      = np.diff(y_hist, axis=0)             # daily changes, (T, n_tau)
_, s, Vt= np.linalg.svd(dy - dy.mean(0), full_matrices=False)
print((s**2 / (s**2).sum())[:4])              # variance shares
# Vt[:3] should look like level / slope / curvature.  If it does not,
# the curve is telling you something the parametric model is hiding.

Shocks are held fixed: a slider transforms the same realisation. \(n\) rebuilds the hedge weights, \(\epsilon\) leaks factor risk back in, the \(\sigma\)s rescale their own components, and \(z_{\rm in},z_{\rm stop},\theta,c,L\) only change what is done with an unchanged path. Exit at \(|z|\le0.25\), at the stop, at the equity barrier, or at the one-year horizon.

What hedging actually buys you

The same signal, the same draws, the same trading rule — changing only how many factors are neutralised. This is the single most important picture on the page.

bars: dispersion \(\sigma(R)\) · dots: mean \(R\) · labels: \(\mathrm{E}[R]/\sigma(R)\)

Unhedged, the residual signal is a rounding error on a rates position and the ratio is indistinguishable from zero. Each factor neutralised removes a large block of variance while leaving the mean essentially untouched, which is the entire economic argument for the strategy: hedging does not create the return, it reveals it. But the improvement stops. Push \(\epsilon\) away from zero and watch the ordering degrade from the right — a three-factor hedge with a 10% weight error can be worse than a clean one-factor hedge, because you have added three noisy legs to remove risk you did not fully remove.

Note also what does not improve: the wipeout probability falls much more slowly than the variance, because forced exits are driven by the worst few days rather than by average dispersion, and the worst few days are exactly when factor loadings stop behaving. Hedging is a claim about the covariance structure, and covariance structures are least reliable precisely when you are relying on them.

The distribution of outcomes

Distribution of \(R = L\cdot\Pi\) over one year (% on equity)

The familiar shape: a tight, high-frequency cluster of small gains and a thin, long left tail. Duarte, Longstaff and Yu (2007) examined precisely this question across fixed-income arbitrage strategies and found that yield-curve and swap-spread strategies do exhibit significant negative skew — but also that, unlike some of their neighbours, the returns are not purely a premium for tail risk. There is genuine intellectual value added in the fitting and the hedging. The nickels are real; so is the steamroller.

How much leverage?

A hedged spread worth a few basis points is not a business at 1×. Since leverage moves the barrier without touching the path, the sweep is exact on the same draws.

Wipeout probability and expected return vs leverage
P(wipeout), left axis E[R], right axis current \(L\)

Same shape as every other levered convergence trade, with one addition worth noticing: the optimum here is a function of \(n\). A better hedge shrinks the distribution, which pushes the peak to higher leverage — and that is how a risk-reducing innovation reliably ends up increasing systemic risk. Nobody uses a better hedge to run the same position more safely; they use it to run a bigger position at the same measured risk.

When the curve stops behaving

  • 1998

    LTCM's on-the-run / off-the-run Treasury trade was the archetype: buy the cheap seasoned bond, short the rich benchmark, collect the liquidity premium as the benchmark seasons. In the flight to quality after the Russian default the premium widened instead. The relationship was not wrong, and it did eventually converge — for whoever bought the positions.

  • 2008–09

    Long-dated swap spreads turned negative, an outcome many models had treated as close to impossible since it appears to imply the government funding above the swap curve. It was a balance-sheet phenomenon, not a credit one. Any fair-value model that hard-codes a sign constraint will report an infinitely attractive trade at exactly the moment it should be reporting that its assumptions have failed.

  • Mar 2020

    Off-the-run Treasuries cheapened dramatically against both on-the-runs and futures as relative-value books were unwound into a market with no balance sheet to absorb them. Dealers hit intermediation limits, and the “convergence” leg of every curve trade became a funding problem instead of a pricing one.

  • Recurrent

    Specialness squeezes and settlement fails on individual issues; index-rebalancing and auction-cycle flows that make a bond persistently rich for structural reasons; central bank purchase programmes that remove a maturity bucket from free float entirely. Each produces a large, stable, entirely genuine residual that will never converge, because it is not a mispricing — it is a price.

Which is the standing discipline of the trade: before putting on a position because a bond is cheap, be able to say who is making it cheap and why they will stop. A residual with no story behind it is a fitting error, and fitting errors do not mean-revert on any schedule you can finance.
