Basis Trade

The basis is the difference between the price of a futures contract and the price of the underlying asset in the cash market: \begin{equation} B_t = F_t - S_t . \end{equation} Under the cost-of-carry model, absence of arbitrage pins the fair futures price to \begin{equation} F_t = S_t\, e^{(r - y)(T - t)}, \end{equation} where \(r\) is the financing (repo) rate, \(y\) the yield earned by holding the asset (coupon, dividend, convenience yield), and \(T\) the delivery date. The fair basis is therefore \begin{equation} B_t^{\text{fair}} = S_t\left(e^{(r - y)(T - t)} - 1\right), \end{equation} and, crucially, \(B_T = 0\): the basis must converge to zero at expiry, since at delivery the futures and the cash asset are the same thing.

The trade

A cash-and-carry basis trade exploits deviations of the market basis from its fair value. If futures are rich (\(B_0 > B_0^{\text{fair}}\)), one:

  • buys the asset in the cash market (financing it via repo at rate \(r\)),
  • sells the futures contract,
  • holds to delivery, where convergence locks in the profit.

The (per-unit) payoff at expiry is deterministic under the model: \begin{equation} \Pi = F_0 - S_0\, e^{(r - y)T}, \end{equation} i.e. the initial net basis. Because \(\Pi\) is typically tiny relative to \(S_0\), the trade is run with leverage \(L\), posting equity \(E = S_0 / L\). The levered return is \begin{equation} R = L\,\frac{\Pi}{S_0}, \end{equation} but leverage cuts both ways: if the mark-to-market value \(V_t\) of the position falls below \(-E\) before expiry, the position is stopped out (a margin call) and the loss is realised before convergence can rescue it. This is exactly the mechanism behind famous basis-trade blow-ups.

A small stochastic model

To visualise the risk, model the spot as a geometric Brownian motion and the basis mispricing \(\varepsilon_t\) as a mean-reverting (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) process forced to vanish at expiry: \begin{align} dS_t &= \mu S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t, \\ d\varepsilon_t &= -\kappa\, \varepsilon_t\, dt + \sigma_b\, dZ_t, \\ F_t &= S_t\, e^{(r - y)(T - t)}\; e^{\varepsilon_t \frac{T-t}{T}} . \end{align} The factor \(\tfrac{T-t}{T}\) guarantees convergence \(F_T = S_T\) regardless of the noise. The mark-to-market value of the (unit-notional) trade — long cash financed at repo, short one future — is \begin{equation} V_t = \underbrace{S_t\,e^{y t} - S_0\, e^{r t}}_{\text{cash leg net of financing}} \;+\; \underbrace{F_0 - F_t}_{\text{short futures leg}} , \end{equation} and the position is knocked out the first time \(V_t < -S_0/L\).

Running many paths shows the classic basis-trade profile: a distribution with a sharp spike at the small, near-deterministic convergence profit, and a left tail of forced liquidations whose weight grows with \(\sigma_b\) and \(L\) — “picking up nickels in front of a steamroller”.

Basis Trade

Two prices for the same asset should agree by delivery day. When they don’t, the gap between them — the basis — is either free money or a very patient way to lose your shirt. Here is the arithmetic, and the risk hiding inside it.

futures Ft spot St basis Bt = Ft − St
t = today → T = delivery 182 days to expiry

§1

Defining the basis

For any asset with a futures contract — a Treasury note, a barrel of oil, an equity index — two prices exist at once: the spot price you would pay to own it now, and the futures price you lock in today for delivery later. The basis is simply the difference.

Definition 1 — Basis
\begin{equation} B_t = F_t - S_t . \end{equation}

At the instant a futures contract expires it converges to spot — delivery forces it to. So the basis is a number engineered to hit exactly zero on a known date. That is what makes basis trades feel like arbitrage: you are not betting on where the market goes, but on a difference collapsing to a value you already know.

§2

Why the basis isn’t zero today

If you could buy the asset now and hold it for free, spot and futures would already be equal. They aren’t, because holding costs something (financing) and sometimes pays something back (dividends, coupons, convenience yield). This is the cost-of-carry model.

Definition 2 — Cost of carry
\begin{equation} F_t = S_t\, e^{(r - y)(T - t)} . \end{equation}
\(r\) = financing (repo) rate · \(y\) = yield earned while holding the asset · \(T-t\) = time to delivery.

Rearranged, the “fair” basis is just the net cost of carrying the asset to delivery.

Corollary 2.1 — Fair basis
\begin{equation} B_t^{\text{fair}} = S_t\left(e^{(r - y)(T - t)} - 1\right), \end{equation}
and crucially \(B_T = 0\): at delivery the futures and the cash asset are the same thing.

When the market’s basis drifts away from this fair value, a mechanical trade appears: buy the cheap side, sell the expensive side, hold to delivery, collect the difference.

§3

The trade itself

The classic version is cash-and-carry: futures are rich (\(B_0 > B_0^{\text{fair}}\)), so you buy the underlying and sell the future against it, financing the purchase until delivery.

Long leg — buy spot

Borrow cash at rate \(r\), buy the asset today at \(S_0\). You own it and owe the loan; at \(T\) you repay \(S_0 e^{rT}\) and deliver the asset.

Short leg — sell the future

Sell a futures contract at \(F_0\), obligating you to deliver the asset at \(T\) for a price fixed today. No cash up front — futures are margined.

Net it out and the profit is locked in at inception, regardless of where the market goes between.

Proposition 3 — Locked-in profit
\begin{equation} \Pi = F_0 - S_0\, e^{(r - y)T}, \end{equation}
the initial net basis. Because \(\Pi\) is tiny relative to \(S_0\), the trade is run with leverage \(L\), posting equity \(E = S_0/L\); the levered return is \(R = L\,\Pi/S_0\).

That is the textbook version. In practice you do not hold to delivery in peace — you hold through a path, and the path is where the risk actually lives.

§4

Two legs, two ways to be forced out

The two legs are financed in completely different ways, and that asymmetry is the source of a second, independent failure mode that the margin-call story alone misses.

Long asset — financed in repo

The bond secures its own financing: you repo it out, pledging the bond as collateral for the cash that bought it. You post equity only to cover the haircut — typically 1–3% of value. But the lender can raise that haircut or refuse to roll: a funding shock.

Short future — margined in cash

The clearing house marks this leg to market daily and demands cash variation margin as the position moves against you. This is the channel behind the mark-to-market knockout \(V_t < -S_0/L\).

So there are two distinct ways the trade dies before convergence can rescue it: a basis-driven margin call (the mark falls past your equity), and a repo funding shock (the lender pulls financing regardless of where the basis is). March 2020 was largely the second channel: repo funding for Treasuries became scarce all at once, forcing leveraged basis trades out on a funding event rather than a pricing one.

§5

The path is stochastic — simulate it

Model the spot as a geometric Brownian motion and the basis mispricing \(\varepsilon_t\) as a mean-reverting (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) process forced to vanish at expiry:

Model 5.1 — Price & basis dynamics
\begin{align} dS_t &= \mu S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t, \\ d\varepsilon_t &= -\kappa\, \varepsilon_t\, dt + \sigma_b\, dZ_t, \\ F_t &= S_t\, e^{(r - y)(T - t)}\; e^{\varepsilon_t \frac{T-t}{T}} . \end{align}
The damping factor \(\tfrac{T-t}{T}\) guarantees convergence \(F_T = S_T\) regardless of the noise.

On top of this pricing dynamics we overlay an independent funding-shock channel: in each step of length \(dt\), the repo lender pulls financing — forcing an immediate unwind at the prevailing mark \(V_t\) — with probability

Model 5.2 — Funding shocks
\begin{equation} \mathbb{P}(\text{funding shock in } [t, t+dt]) = 1 - e^{-h\,dt} \approx h\,dt, \end{equation}
\(h\) = funding-stress rate (expected shocks/yr). This event is independent of the basis level — it can fire even when the trade is in profit.

§7

When the “arbitrage” breaks

Cash-and-carry only looks riskless if you can fund the position all the way to delivery. Two episodes show what happens when that assumption fails — one dominated by each channel above.

  • LTCM, 1998. Long-Term Capital Management ran highly levered basis and relative-value trades across bond markets. When Russia defaulted and spreads widened instead of converging, mark-to-market margin calls forced the fund to unwind at the worst possible moment — turning positions that were “right” on paper into realised losses. This is the basis-driven margin-call channel.
  • Treasury basis, March 2020. Hedge funds running the cash–futures basis trade at high leverage were caught when the COVID shock hit. Crucially, the binding constraint was largely funding: repo financing for Treasuries became scarce and dealer balance sheets filled, so the repo leg — not the pricing leg — forced deleveraging, amplifying the sell-off in a market usually considered among the safest in the world. This is the funding-shock channel [2], [3].

The lesson isn’t that the math is wrong — convergence at delivery is close to certain. It’s that leverage turns a small, temporary, adverse move — or a purely mechanical loss of financing — into a forced exit, and forced exits happen before the “sure thing” gets to pay off.
