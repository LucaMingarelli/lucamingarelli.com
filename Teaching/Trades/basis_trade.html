Basis Trade

The basis is the difference between the price of a futures contract and the price of the underlying asset in the cash market: \begin{equation} B_t = F_t - S_t . \end{equation} Under the cost-of-carry model, absence of arbitrage pins the fair futures price to \begin{equation} F_t = S_t\, e^{(r - y)(T - t)}, \end{equation} where \(r\) is the financing (repo) rate, \(y\) the yield earned by holding the asset (coupon, dividend, convenience yield), and \(T\) the delivery date. The fair basis is therefore \begin{equation} B_t^{\text{fair}} = S_t\left(e^{(r - y)(T - t)} - 1\right), \end{equation} and, crucially, \(B_T = 0\): the basis must converge to zero at expiry, since at delivery the futures and the cash asset are the same thing.

The trade

A cash-and-carry basis trade exploits deviations of the market basis from its fair value. If futures are rich (\(B_0 > B_0^{\text{fair}}\)), one:

  • buys the asset in the cash market (financing it via repo at rate \(r\)),
  • sells the futures contract,
  • holds to delivery, where convergence locks in the profit.

To make the position deliver exactly one unit at expiry, buy \(n = e^{-yT}\) units at inception and reinvest the income; holdings then grow to \(n\,e^{yT} = 1\). The per-unit payoff at expiry is deterministic under the model: \begin{equation} \Pi = F_0 - S_0\, e^{(r - y)T}, \end{equation} i.e. the initial net basis. Because \(\Pi\) is typically tiny relative to \(S_0\), the trade is run with leverage \(L\), posting equity \(E = S_0 / L\). The levered return is \begin{equation} R = L\,\frac{\Pi}{S_0}, \end{equation} but leverage cuts both ways: if the mark-to-market value \(V_t\) falls below \(-E\) before expiry, the position is stopped out (a margin call) and the loss is realised before convergence can rescue it. Note that this barrier is, by construction, the total loss of your equity: a forced exit is a \(-100\%\) return. This is exactly the mechanism behind famous basis-trade blow-ups.

A small stochastic model

To visualise the risk, model the spot as a geometric Brownian motion and the basis mispricing \(\varepsilon_t\) as a mean-reverting (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) process forced to vanish at expiry: \begin{align} dS_t &= \mu S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t, \\ d\varepsilon_t &= -\kappa\, \varepsilon_t\, dt + \sigma_b\, dZ_t, \\ F_t &= S_t\, e^{(r - y)(T - t)}\; e^{\varepsilon_t \frac{T-t}{T}} . \end{align} The factor \(\tfrac{T-t}{T}\) guarantees convergence \(F_T = S_T\) regardless of the noise. The mark-to-market value of the trade — \(n\) units of the asset financed at repo, short one future — is \begin{equation} V_t = \underbrace{n\left(S_t\,e^{y t} - S_0\, e^{r t}\right)}_{\text{cash leg net of financing}} \;+\; \underbrace{F_0 - F_t}_{\text{short futures leg}} , \qquad n = e^{-yT}, \end{equation} so that \(V_T = F_0 - S_0 e^{(r-y)T} = \Pi\) exactly, and the position is knocked out the first time \(V_t < -S_0/L\).

Play with the parameters below: note how the final P&L does not change at all (convergence is a certainty in this model) while the path of the P&L — and hence the probability of a margin call — depends strongly on basis volatility \(\sigma_b\), reversion speed \(\kappa\) and leverage \(L\).

Why the survivor spike is a single bar. Under this model the cash-and-carry is a genuine static arbitrage: every path that reaches delivery earns exactly \(\Pi = S_0 e^{(r-y)T}\left(e^{\varepsilon_0}-1\right)\), with no dispersion whatsoever. That is a property of the model (perfect convergence, constant financing, no delivery option), not of reality — for Treasury futures the cheapest-to-deliver option and repo-rate variability would smear it out. The spike is clipped in the chart so the tail stays visible; its true count is printed above it.
