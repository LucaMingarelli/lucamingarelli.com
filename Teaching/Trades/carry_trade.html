The Carry Trade

Borrow where money is cheap, lend where it is dear, and pocket the difference. Theory says the exchange rate should move exactly enough to take that difference back. It does not — which leaves either a free lunch or a bill that arrives all at once.

The trade

Two currencies, two interest rates. Borrow the low-rate one, convert, deposit in the high-rate one. Every day that passes you earn the difference; the only thing that can hurt you is the exchange rate.

Definition 1 — Carry and excess return
Let \(s_t\) be the log price of the investment currency in units of the funding currency, and \(\delta = i_{\rm inv}-i_{\rm fund}>0\) the interest differential. The excess return over \([t,t+dt]\) is \begin{equation} dr_t \;=\; \underbrace{\delta\,dt}_{\text{carry}} \;+\; \underbrace{ds_t}_{\text{currency}} . \end{equation}
\(s\) up = investment currency appreciates = the carry trader gains

The first term is known in advance and accrues smoothly. The second is unknown and moves a great deal. A 4% differential is 1.6 basis points a day; the currency routinely moves fifty times that before lunch. The entire question is whether the second term has a mean of zero.

Funding leg — short the low-yielder

Borrow yen, francs, euros, or in the modern form simply sell them forward. You owe \(i_{\rm fund}\) and you are short a currency that tends to be a safe haven.

pay \(i_{\rm fund}\) · short a currency that rallies in crises
Investment leg — long the high-yielder

Hold real, peso, lira, rupiah. You earn \(i_{\rm inv}\), and you are long a currency whose high rate is frequently compensation for something.

earn \(i_{\rm inv}\) · long a currency that gaps in crises

In practice nobody moves cash. The trade is a forward or a non-deliverable forward, and the differential shows up as forward points rather than as interest. That equivalence is not a convention — it is enforced by arbitrage, and it is the first of our two parities.

Two parities, one of which is a theorem

These are constantly confused, and the difference between them is the whole subject.

Definition 2 — Covered interest parity (CIP)
\begin{equation} f_t - s_t \;=\; i_{\rm fund} - i_{\rm inv} \;=\; -\delta , \end{equation} with \(f_t\) the log forward rate for the same horizon.
an arbitrage identity: violating it is a riskless money machine
Definition 3 — Uncovered interest parity (UIP)
\begin{equation} \mathbb E_t[s_{t+1}-s_t] \;=\; -\delta \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mathbb E_t[s_{t+1}] = f_t . \end{equation}
a hypothesis about expectations: violating it is merely being wrong

CIP is a statement about prices that exist simultaneously, and it is enforceable by anyone with a balance sheet. UIP is a statement about the future, equivalent to saying the forward rate is an unbiased forecast of the spot rate. It is not an arbitrage condition, it is a joint hypothesis about rational expectations and risk-neutrality, and it fails comprehensively.

Definition 4 — The Fama regression
\begin{equation} s_{t+1}-s_t \;=\; \alpha \;-\; \varphi\,\delta_t \;+\; \varepsilon_{t+1} . \end{equation} UIP predicts \(\varphi = 1\): the high-yielder depreciates by exactly its yield advantage.
\(\varphi=0\): a random walk, and carry is earned in full · \(\varphi<0\): the high-yielder appreciates on average

Fama (1984) and a very large subsequent literature find \(\hat\varphi\) reliably below one and frequently negative. High-interest-rate currencies do not fall as UIP requires; on average, over long samples, they have tended to rise slightly. This is the forward premium puzzle, and it means the carry trade earns not just the differential but a little more.

Note the two fitted lines. Dropping the handful of crash observations makes the puzzle look dramatically worse — which is the first hint that the crashes are not a nuisance in the data, they are the point of it.

CIP is a theorem, but not a law of nature. Since 2008 persistent cross-currency bases have opened up: \(f_t-s_t-(i_{\rm fund}-i_{\rm inv})\neq 0\), sometimes by tens of basis points and reliably widening at quarter-ends. Du, Tepper and Verdelhan (2018) document this in detail. The arbitrage did not stop being an arbitrage; balance sheet became scarce and expensive, so nobody sufficiently large found it worth doing. Keep the distinction in mind: CIP now fails for regulatory reasons, UIP fails for economic ones.

The shape of the return

A positive average return with a well-behaved distribution would be a puzzle. What carry actually delivers is a positive average return with a distribution that is anything but.

Skewnessstrongly negative Many small gains, occasional very large losses. The Sharpe ratio is the wrong statistic.

The desk aphorism is that carry goes up by the stairs and down by the elevator. Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen (2008) made this precise: currencies with high interest differentials exhibit significant negative skewness, and the crashes coincide with spikes in volatility and with reductions in speculator positioning — a liquidity spiral in which losses force unwinds which cause further losses.

Why does it pay?

Four families of explanation, not mutually exclusive, and the honest position is that the profession has not settled the weights.

1. Crash-risk premium

The return compensates for rare, large, correlated losses. Farhi and Gabaix (2016) build this from rare disasters; high-yield currencies are those more exposed to a global disaster, and are paid for it. Under this reading carry is not an anomaly at all.

2. The peso problem

The sample simply does not contain the devaluation that everyone rationally feared. Named for the Mexican peso's long forward discount before the 1976 float. Burnside et al. (2011) argue peso events alone do not account for the returns.

3. Priced risk factors

Lustig, Roussanov and Verdelhan (2011) show a slope factor prices the cross-section of currency returns; Menkhoff et al. (2012) show carry loads negatively on innovations to global FX volatility. Carry pays because it loses when volatility spikes.

4. Limits to arbitrage

The trade requires leverage, funding, and the ability to survive drawdowns. Capital is slow and intermediaries are constrained, so the premium persists because the marginal investor cannot hold enough of it.

There is a clean empirical test buried in explanation 1. If the return is purely crash compensation, then hedging the crash with out-of-the-money options should remove it. Jurek (2014) constructs exactly these crash-neutral portfolios; the hedged returns are reduced but a substantial part survives, which argues against crash risk being the whole story. The simulator below lets you dial the parameters to whichever of these worlds you find plausible — and prints how much of the carry the jump risk is eating.

A small stochastic model

\(N\) investment currencies against one funding currency, equally weighted, each with a common factor, an idiosyncratic factor, and jumps that are partly shared:

\begin{align} ds_i &= -\varphi\,\delta\,dt + \sigma_c\,dW_c + \sigma_x\,dW_i - \jmath\,(dN_c + dN_i),\\ dr_i &= \delta\,dt + ds_i , \qquad\qquad R = \tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_i r_i . \end{align}

\(N_c\) is a common Poisson process with intensity \(h_c\) — when it fires, every currency drops by \(\jmath\) at once — while each \(N_i\) is idiosyncratic with intensity \(h_x\). Aggregating the portfolio,

\begin{equation} dR = \delta(1-\varphi)\,dt \;+\; \sigma_c\,dW_c \;+\; \frac{\sigma_x}{\sqrt N}\,d\bar W \;-\; \jmath\,dN_c \;-\; \frac{\jmath}{N}\,dK_t , \qquad K_t\sim\text{Bin}(N,h_x dt), \end{equation}

which displays the central fact immediately: the \(1/\sqrt N\) and \(1/N\) terms vanish with diversification and the \(dN_c\) term does not. The expected annual return and the crash-risk share of it are

\begin{equation} \mathbb E[R] = \delta(1-\varphi) - \jmath\,(h_c + h_x), \qquad \text{crash share} = \frac{\jmath\,(h_c+h_x)}{\delta(1-\varphi)} . \end{equation}

When the crash share reaches one, the entire carry is a fair premium for jump risk and the strategy has zero expected return: the puzzle is fully resolved by the peso story, and what remains is a lottery sold at fair value. Watch that readout as you move the sliders.

Finally, leverage. Equity is \(1/L\) of notional and the position is wiped out the first time cumulative \(R_t \le -1/L\). Since \(L\) moves only the barrier and never the path, the per-path running minima are recorded once and the whole leverage sweep is exact. 

import numpy as np, statsmodels.api as sm

# --- the Fama regression, stated in the page's sign convention --------------
# s = log price of the INVESTMENT currency in units of the FUNDING currency
# delta = i_inv - i_fund > 0
ds    = np.diff(np.log(spot))
X     = sm.add_constant(-delta[:-1])          # note the minus: phi is the slope on -delta
fit   = sm.OLS(ds, X).fit()
phi   = fit.params[1]
print(f"phi = {phi:+.2f}   (UIP predicts +1.00)")

# --- the statistic that actually matters ------------------------------------
r     = delta[:-1]/252 + ds                   # daily excess return of the carry trade
from scipy.stats import skew
print(f"Sharpe {r.mean()/r.std()*np.sqrt(252):.2f}   skew {skew(r):+.2f}")

The distribution of outcomes

Distribution of \(R = L\cdot\Pi\) over one year (% on equity)

The mode sits comfortably to the right of zero and the mean sits well to the left of the mode. That gap is the entire risk of the strategy, and it is invisible in any statistic built from the first two moments. Set \(h_c=h_x=0\) and the histogram becomes symmetric and the Sharpe ratio becomes absurd — which is precisely the illusion a short sample of a jump process produces.

The default parameters are calibrated to be defensible rather than flattering: a 5% differential, roughly one systemic crash per decade, and \(\varphi=0\) — the conservative random-walk case rather than the negative \(\varphi\) the data actually show. That yields about 3% a year unlevered at a Sharpe near 0.4, with a third of the gross carry consumed by jump risk. Watch the crash share readout above the charts: when it passes 100% the differential no longer covers the expected jump loss, expected return turns negative, and what still looks like a healthy histogram is nothing but a positive median attached to a negative mean. The peso problem preset puts you there deliberately.

Diversification cannot save you

The natural response to a skewed single-pair trade is to run twenty of them. Here is what that buys, on the same draws, varying only \(N\).

Volatility and skewness vs number of currencies
\(\sigma(R)\), left axis undiversifiable floor skewness, right axis current \(N\)

Volatility drops quickly and then stops, pinned by \(\sigma_c\) and the common jump. Skewness does the opposite: it gets worse. Averaging strips out the idiosyncratic noise that was partly masking the common crash, so what remains is a cleaner, more concentrated exposure to the one thing you cannot diversify. The portfolio looks safer by every symmetric measure while becoming a purer bet on the single event that kills it.

This is the mechanism behind the recurring observation that carry unwinds hit every pair simultaneously. Diversification across currencies is diversification across the idiosyncratic component only, and the idiosyncratic component was never the risk. Worse, a volatility-targeted book increases leverage as \(N\) rises, precisely offsetting the apparent risk reduction.

The usual hump, with one carry-specific wrinkle: because the loss arrives as a jump rather than as a diffusion, the barrier can be breached without ever being approached. A stop-loss offers far less protection than it does in a mean-reverting trade — you do not get to exit at your stop, you get to exit at whatever price exists after the gap. Retail FX brokers discovered this collectively in January 2015.

When the elevator arrives

  • 1992

    The ERM crisis. Pegged and semi-pegged currencies offering visible interest premia are the purest form of the trade: the carry is high precisely because the peg might break. Sterling left the mechanism in September and the accumulated carry of years was returned in days.

  • Oct 1998

    The yen appreciated violently against the dollar over a couple of days — figures around 15% are widely cited — as LTCM-related deleveraging forced the unwinding of yen-funded positions across the market. No macroeconomic news of comparable size occurred. It was a positioning event.

  • 2008

    The global crisis produced a textbook carry crash: funding currencies rallied hard, high yielders collapsed, and the losses were near-perfectly correlated across pairs. This episode is the empirical backbone of the global-volatility explanation.

  • Jan 2015

    The Swiss National Bank abandoned the EUR/CHF floor without warning. The franc appreciated by roughly 30% intraday. Stops did not fill, several retail brokers became insolvent, and one of the largest required emergency financing. The distribution of a pegged currency is not merely fat-tailed, it is bimodal, and no volatility estimate computed under the peg contained any information about the second mode.

  • Aug 2024

    A Bank of Japan rate rise and a weak US payrolls print combined to trigger a rapid unwind of yen-funded positions. The yen surged, and on 5 August the Nikkei recorded its largest single-day percentage fall since 1987. A reminder that the funding leg transmits stress into every asset the trade was financing.

The pattern is consistent. The carry trade is not wrong — the differential is real, it accrues daily, and over long samples it more than compensates. What it is, is a position whose losses are concentrated, correlated and immediate, held by people whose capital is leveraged, and therefore unwound at exactly the moment the unwinding is most expensive. The elevator is not a flaw in the trade. It is the trade.

Carry is not just an FX trade

Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen and Vrugt (2018) generalise the definition: the carry of any asset is its expected return assuming its price does not change. Under that definition the same strategy exists everywhere.

They find carry strategies earn positive returns across all of these, with correlated drawdowns in bad times. That last clause is the important one: the payoffs look independent until they are not, and a portfolio built from six carry strategies across six markets is far less diversified than its covariance matrix says.
