Pairs Trading

Two assets that move together are not the same thing as two assets that stay together. The first is correlation, and it is nearly useless on its own. The second is cointegration, and it is the only version of the story that has a trade in it.

The relative-value idea

A directional trade needs a view on where a price is going. A relative-value trade needs only a view on where a difference is going, and difference is a much smaller thing to have an opinion about. Buy one asset, short a matched amount of a second, and what remains is a single number: the spread.

Definition 1 — Spread and hedge ratio
\begin{equation} s_t = y_t - \alpha - \beta\,x_t, \qquad x_t=\log P^A_t,\;\; y_t=\log P^B_t . \end{equation}
\(\beta\) = hedge ratio · work in logs so \(\beta\) is a ratio of exposures, not of currency amounts

The whole edifice rests on one assumption: that \(s_t\) is a stationary object, something with a mean it keeps coming back to. If that is true, then a large \(|s_t|\) is information, and the trade writes itself: sell the rich leg, buy the cheap leg, wait. If it is false, a large \(|s_t|\) is just a large number, and waiting is not a strategy.

Everything below is about how to tell those two situations apart, and about the ways the test can lie to you.

Why correlation is the wrong tool

Correlation is computed on returns, over short horizons, after differencing away exactly the thing a pairs trader cares about — the level. Take two independent random walks and correlate their increments at \(\rho\). The spread \(s_t=y_t-x_t\) is then itself a random walk:

Proposition 2 — Correlated is not converging
\begin{equation} \operatorname{Var}(s_t)=\big(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_y^2-2\rho\,\sigma_x\sigma_y\big)\,t \;\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\;\infty . \end{equation}
the variance rate vanishes only in the degenerate case \(\rho=1,\ \sigma_x=\sigma_y\)

High correlation shrinks the constant; it does not change the \(t\). With \(\rho=0.95\) and \(\sigma=25\%\), the gap still has a standard deviation of about \(8\%\) after one year and \(25\%\) after nine — and no force whatsoever pulling it back. A trader who shorted that spread at \(2\sigma\) is not early, they are simply wrong, and their position size was chosen by a statistic that never made the claim they thought it made.

Correlation — a statement about returns

Short-horizon, scale-free, computed after differencing. Unstable across regimes, and entirely silent about the level of the gap. Two assets can be \(0.99\) correlated and drift arbitrarily far apart.

\(\operatorname{Var}(s_t)\propto t\) · no anchor · no half-life
Cointegration — a statement about levels

Long-horizon. Asserts that a specific linear combination is bounded in probability: it has a finite unconditional variance and an error-correction mechanism that restores it.

\(\operatorname{Var}(s_t)\to\sigma_s^2/2\kappa\) · anchored · half-life \(\ln 2/\kappa\)

And the converse also fails, which is the part people find surprising: a genuinely cointegrated pair can have quite modest return correlation, because a fast, noisy, mean-reverting spread adds idiosyncratic variance to the daily changes while leaving the long-run relationship intact. Correlation and cointegration are close to independent properties.

Spurious regression. Regress one random walk on an unrelated one and you will typically get a large \(t\)-statistic and a flattering \(R^2\); Granger and Newbold (1974) showed the rejection rate of “no relationship” approaches one as the sample grows. The usual \(t\)-tables assume stationary residuals, which is precisely what is in question. So an impressive-looking regression of two price levels is evidence of nothing until the residual has been tested for a unit root.

Cointegration

The formal apparatus is Engle and Granger's. Start with the order of integration.

Definition 3 — Integration of order \(d\)
A series \(x_t\) is \(I(d)\) if \((1-L)^d x_t\) is stationary while \((1-L)^{d-1}x_t\) is not. Log prices are typically \(I(1)\); log returns are \(I(0)\).
Definition 4 — Cointegration
The vector \(\mathbf{z}_t=(x_t,y_t)'\) of \(I(1)\) series is cointegrated if there exists \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\neq\mathbf 0\) with \begin{equation} \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf z_t \sim I(0). \end{equation} \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is the cointegrating vector; it is identified only up to scale, so one normalises \(\boldsymbol{\beta}=(-\beta,1)'\).

The economic content is that the two series share a common stochastic trend. Each wanders without bound, but they wander together, and the particular combination \(y_t-\beta x_t\) cancels the trend exactly. Not approximately — exactly. This is a knife-edge property, which is why it needs a reason to hold: the same cash flows in two listings, a commodity and its refined product, an ETF and its basket, two points on the same curve, two firms with the same input costs and the same customers.

The reason this is tradable, rather than merely true, is the Granger representation theorem.

Theorem 5 — Granger representation
If \(\mathbf z_t\) is cointegrated, it admits a vector error-correction representation \begin{equation} \Delta \mathbf z_t=\underbrace{\boldsymbol\alpha\,\big(\boldsymbol\beta'\mathbf z_{t-1}\big)}_{\text{error correction}} +\sum_{i=1}^{p}\boldsymbol\Gamma_i\,\Delta\mathbf z_{t-i}+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t , \end{equation} with \(\boldsymbol\alpha\neq\mathbf 0\), and conversely.
\(\boldsymbol\alpha\) = loadings, the speed at which each leg absorbs the disequilibrium

Read the first term slowly: yesterday's spread predicts today's returns, with a coefficient that is not zero by construction. Cointegration is therefore not a statistical curiosity but an explicit statement of return predictability, and it says something extra that a pure spread model does not — the loadings tell you which leg does the adjusting. If \(\alpha_x\approx0\), asset A is the price leader and B is the one that comes back; that is useful for choosing which side to size up, and for guessing what happens when the relationship stops working.

Estimating and testing

Two standard routes. The Engle–Granger two-step is a regression on levels followed by a unit-root test on the residual; the Johansen procedure estimates the whole VECM by maximum likelihood and tests the rank of \(\boldsymbol{\alpha\beta}'\), which is what you need as soon as there are more than two assets.

StepWhat you computeWhat can go wrong
1. Hedge ratioOLS of \(y_t\) on \(x_t\) in levels Superconsistent, but badly biased in small samples; the answer differs depending on which series you put on the left.
2. Residual testADF on \(\hat s_t\) Because \(\beta\) was estimated, Dickey–Fuller critical values are too loose; use Phillips–Ouliaris.
3. Dynamics\(\kappa\) from \(\Delta \hat s_t=-\kappa\,\hat s_{t-1}\Delta t+\epsilon_t\) Half-life estimates are noisy and biased towards fast reversion in short samples.
4. RankJohansen trace / max-eigenvalue Sensitive to lag length and to the deterministic-trend specification.
import numpy as np, statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import coint

x, y = np.log(px_a), np.log(px_b)

# Engle-Granger step 1: cointegrating vector by OLS on LEVELS
res          = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
alpha, beta  = res.params
spread       = y - alpha - beta * x

# step 2: unit-root test on the residual.  coint() applies the Phillips-Ouliaris
# critical values, which account for beta having been estimated -- adfuller() does not.
tstat, pval, crit = coint(y, x, trend='c')

# speed of mean reversion  ->  half-life
ds     = np.diff(spread)
kappa  = -sm.OLS(ds, sm.add_constant(spread[:-1])).fit().params[1] * 252
half_life = np.log(2) / kappa            # in years

# sanity: is the relationship stable?  re-estimate on rolling windows and look at beta.
The multiple-testing problem is the real problem. A universe of 500 stocks contains \(\binom{500}{2}=124{,}750\) pairs. Testing all of them at the 5% level produces roughly 6,200 “cointegrated” pairs under the null that none of them are. Any search procedure that ranks pairs by \(p\)-value is a machine for manufacturing false positives, and the false positives are exactly the pairs whose spread happened to look most stationary in-sample — which is to say, the ones that will look most attractive and behave worst. Economic rationale first, statistics second; then out-of-sample, on a period the search never saw.

From a stationary spread to a trade

Model the spread as Ornstein–Uhlenbeck. Two numbers then summarise everything a trader needs: how far it typically strays, and how long it takes to come back.

Definition 6 — Equilibrium scale and half-life
\begin{equation} ds_t=-\kappa\,s_t\,dt+\sigma_s\,dZ_t,\qquad \sigma_{\rm eq}=\frac{\sigma_s}{\sqrt{2\kappa}},\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{\kappa}. \end{equation}
\(\sigma_{\rm eq}\) = unconditional s.d. of the spread · \(t_{1/2}\) = time for a deviation to decay by half

The half-life is the parameter that decides whether the pair is worth trading at all. A spread with \(t_{1/2}\) of three days is a market-making problem dominated by transaction costs; one with \(t_{1/2}\) of two years is a capital-allocation problem you will not survive. Somewhere between a week and a couple of months is where the strategy lives.

Definition 7 — The rule
With \(z_t=\tilde s_t/\sigma_{\rm eq}\) and position \(q_t\in\{-1,0,+1\}\) in the spread, \begin{align} \text{enter } q=-1 &\;\text{ when } z_t\ge z_{\rm in}, \qquad \text{enter } q=+1 \;\text{ when } z_t\le -z_{\rm in},\\ \text{exit} &\;\text{ when } |z_t|\le z_{\rm out} \;\text{ (converged)}\\ \text{or} &\;\text{ when } |z_t|\ge z_{\rm stop} \;\text{ (stop)} . \end{align}

The stop is the uncomfortable part. Under the model the spread always comes back, so a stop-loss can only destroy value; in reality the model is an assumption that expires without notice, and the stop is the only thing standing between you and an unbounded loss on a relationship that has quietly stopped existing. Choosing \(z_{\rm stop}\) is choosing how much you believe your own econometrics.

A small stochastic model

One common trend, one stationary spread, and two ways for the trade to be wrong about the world.

\begin{align} dc_t &= \left(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_c^2\right)dt+\sigma_c\,dW_t, \\ x_t &= c_t, \qquad y_t=\alpha+\beta c_t+s_t, \\ ds_t &= -\kappa\,s_t\,dt+\sigma_s\,dZ_t,\qquad s_0\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\tfrac{\sigma_s^2}{2\kappa}\right), \end{align}

so the pair is cointegrated with vector \((-\beta,1)'\) by construction. Now trade it with an estimated hedge ratio \(\hat\beta=\beta+\delta\). The spread you actually hold is

\begin{equation} \tilde s_t \;=\; y_t-\hat\alpha-\hat\beta\,x_t \;=\; s_t-\delta\,c_t , \end{equation}

which is \(I(1)\) for any \(\delta\neq0\): the hedge error re-injects the common stochastic trend, and the traded spread inherits a random walk of variance \(\delta^2\sigma_c^2 t\) on top of the mean-reverting part. A 5% error in the hedge ratio does not make the trade 5% worse; it makes it a different trade. This is the single most common way a correct cointegration analysis turns into a losing book, and the slider \(\delta\) below is worth more attention than any of the others.

The second failure is the relationship itself ending. Model it as a Poisson event:

\begin{equation} \mathbb P\big(\text{break in }[t,t+dt]\big)=1-e^{-h\,dt}\approx h\,dt, \qquad \kappa \to 0 \;\text{ thereafter} . \end{equation}

After a break the spread is a driftless random walk. Note what this does and does not do: it does not create a loss by fiat — a random walk is a martingale, so an open position has expected P&L of zero from that point. What it destroys is the edge, while leaving the variance, the costs and the stop-loss fully intact. Broken cointegration does not lose money dramatically; it turns a strategy into a coin flip that pays commission.

Discretised on a daily grid, with round-trip cost \(c\) split half on entry and half on exit:

\begin{equation} s_{i+1}=(1-\kappa\Delta t)\,s_i+\sigma_s\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z_i,\qquad \Pi=\sum_i q_i\,(\tilde s_{i+1}-\tilde s_i)\;-\;c\cdot\#\{\text{round trips}\},\qquad R=L\,\Pi . \end{equation}

\(L\) is the notional of each leg per unit of equity, so \(R\) is a return on capital. Note that \(L\) rescales P&L but cannot change the Sharpe ratio, which is why leverage is reported separately from the risk statistics below.

The distribution of outcomes

Two thousand paths on the same shock buffer. The characteristic shape is the mirror image of the basis trade: a dense cluster of small, repeatable gains from spreads that converged, and a left tail made of the pairs where the relationship stopped holding — either because it broke, or because it was never quite there to begin with (\(\delta\neq0\)).

Distribution of \(R=L\,\Pi\) over one year (% on equity)
This model is far too kind. The trader here knows \(\kappa\), \(\sigma_s\) and \(\sigma_{\rm eq}\) exactly, the spread is exactly Gaussian and exactly OU, there is no borrow cost, no short recall, no gap risk, no crowding and no dividend or corporate-action mess. Set \(\delta=0,h=0\) and the simulated Sharpe is embarrassing to print. Realised Sharpe ratios for cash-equity stat-arb after costs are conventionally reported in the low single digits, and the gap between the two numbers is the subject: it is all parameter uncertainty and structural instability, which is what \(\delta\) and \(h\) are crude stand-ins for.

How far do you let it stretch?

Since the trading rule never touches the simulated paths, the entry threshold can be swept exactly: the same 2,000 spread paths are simply re-read under each candidate \(z_{\rm in}\).

There is a tension in both directions. Enter too eagerly and you pay the round-trip cost constantly for a handful of basis points of expected convergence, and you spend most of the year holding positions whose edge does not cover their variance. Wait too long and the opportunities never arrive — and, worse, the deviations that are large enough to clear a high bar are disproportionately the ones generated by a broken relationship rather than by noise. Raising \(h\), \(c\) or \(|\delta|\) pushes the optimum around; the useful exercise is to watch which way it moves and convince yourself why.

NOTE: as on any expected-value criterion, the peak is risk-neutral. It ignores that the tail outcomes here are correlated across every pair in a real book — when stat-arb spreads stop reverting, they tend to stop reverting simultaneously. The optimum for a single pair is an upper bound for a portfolio of them, not a target.

When the relationship stops existing

  • 1980s

    The technique is generally credited to the quantitative group run by Nunzio Tartaglia at Morgan Stanley, with Gerry Bamberger often named as the originator. The original version was close to pure distance-matching on price series; the econometrics arrived later, and mostly served to explain why the naive version worked when it did.

  • 1907–2005

    Royal Dutch and Shell were the textbook cointegrated pair: two listings on a fixed 60/40 split of the same cash flows, an essentially mechanical parity. Froot and Dabora documented deviations from that parity of tens of percent, persisting for years and tracking the relative performance of the markets in which each line traded. The relationship was real, the arbitrage was real, and it was still capable of running against a levered position for longer than the position could be financed — LTCM among others found this out in 1998.

  • Aug 2007

    The “quant quake”. Over a few days in early August, market-neutral equity strategies suffered severe, highly correlated losses followed by a sharp rebound, with no corresponding move in the index. Khandani and Lo attribute it to the rapid unwind of a large multi-strategy book forcing deleveraging across everyone holding similar spreads. No cointegrating relationship broke: the positions were crowded, and crowding is a risk factor that appears nowhere in an ADF test.

  • Any time

    The mundane ways a pair dies: a merger, a spin-off, a change of index membership, a shift in capital structure, a new competitor, a regulatory change in one jurisdiction only. Each is a structural break, none is forecastable from the price history, and all of them look identical to a large \(z\)-score right up until they don't.

The discipline is therefore the reverse of what the mathematics suggests. Cointegration is what makes the trade possible, but it is an assumption with a shelf life, and the operational job is not finding it — a search will find far more of it than exists — but deciding in advance how you will behave when it is no longer true.
