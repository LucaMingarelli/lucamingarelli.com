Pairs Trading & Cointegration

Two assets that move together are not the same thing as two assets that must come back together. The first is correlation, and it is nearly useless. The second is cointegration, and it is the entire trade.

The idea

A pairs trade is a bet on a relative price, not an absolute one. You find two assets whose prices are tied together by something economic — the same cash flows, the same commodity, the same balance sheet, the same index — and when the tie stretches you sell the expensive one, buy the cheap one, and wait for the tie to pull them back.

Short leg — the rich asset

Sell \(\$1\) of \(A\). You need to borrow it, which costs a fee and can be recalled.

entry: \(+\$1\) cash, short 1 unit of \(A\)
exit: buy back at (hopefully) a lower relative price
Long leg — the cheap asset

Buy \(\$\beta\) of \(B\), where \(\beta\) is the hedge ratio that removes the common factor. Gross notional is \(1+\beta\); net market exposure is (approximately) zero.

entry: \(-\$\beta\) cash, long \(\beta\) units of \(B\)
P&L: \(\;\theta\,(\Delta p^A - \beta\,\Delta p^B)\)

Everything hard about this is in the phrase “pull them back”. The market owes you nothing. What you need is a statistical statement strong enough to justify the word must, and correlation is not that statement.

Why correlation is the wrong tool

The default screen is the correlation of returns.

Definition 1 — Return correlation
\begin{equation} \rho \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\Delta p^A_t,\ \Delta p^B_t\right)} {\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\Delta p^A_t\ \operatorname{Var}\Delta p^B_t}}, \qquad \Delta p_t = p_t - p_{t-1},\quad p=\log P . \end{equation}
a statement about differences, over one period, contemporaneously

Note what the definition throws away: the level. Correlation is computed on returns precisely because returns are (approximately) stationary, and that differencing step destroys exactly the information a pairs trader needs. Two random walks can have a return correlation of \(0.99\) and still see their price gap diverge without bound — the gap is itself a random walk, with variance growing linearly in \(t\). Conversely, a pair can be firmly tethered in levels while its daily returns look almost unrelated, if the tether is fast relative to the common factor.

The spurious regression trap

Suppose you skip correlation and regress one price level on another, \(p^A_t = \alpha + \beta\,p^B_t + e_t\), then look at \(R^2\) and the \(t\)-statistic on \(\hat\beta\). This is worse. Granger and Newbold (1974) showed that regressing one independent random walk on another produces high \(R^2\) and enormous \(t\)-statistics essentially at will: the usual asymptotics fail because the regression error inherits a unit root, so \(t\) diverges like \(\sqrt{n}\) instead of converging to \(\mathcal N(0,1)\).

The practical form of this trap is pair mining. Screening 500 stocks means testing \(\binom{500}{2}=124{,}750\) pairs; a test with 5% size hands you roughly 6,200 false “cointegrated” pairs before a single one of them is economically real. Multiple-testing corrections, or better, an economic prior (dual listings, share classes, index members and their ETF, producer–refiner, issuer curves) is not optional.

Cointegration

The right object was formalised by Engle and Granger (1987). Begin with the order of integration.

Definition 2 — Order of integration

A series \(x_t\) is \(I(d)\) if \(\Delta^d x_t\) is stationary while \(\Delta^{d-1}x_t\) is not. Log prices are typically \(I(1)\); log returns are \(I(0)\).

Definition 3 — Cointegration

Let \(p_t\in\mathbb R^n\) with every component \(I(1)\). The components are cointegrated if there exists \(b\neq 0\) with

\begin{equation} b'p_t \;\sim\; I(0). \end{equation}

\(b\) is a cointegrating vector; the number of linearly independent such vectors is the cointegration rank \(r\).

for a pair, \(b=(1,-\beta)'\) and \(\;e_t=p^A_t-\alpha-\beta p^B_t\) is the tradable spread

Generically, a linear combination of \(I(1)\) series is \(I(1)\); cointegration is a knife-edge cancellation of the stochastic trends, and it is exactly this cancellation that makes the spread forecastable. The economic content is delivered by the representation theorem.

Theorem 4 — Granger representation

\(p_t\) is cointegrated if and only if it admits a vector error-correction representation. For a pair,

\begin{align} \Delta p^A_t &= c_A + \phi_A\, e_{t-1} + \textstyle\sum_j \gamma^A_j \Delta p_{t-j} + \eta^A_t,\\ \Delta p^B_t &= c_B + \phi_B\, e_{t-1} + \textstyle\sum_j \gamma^B_j \Delta p_{t-j} + \eta^B_t, \end{align}

with \(e_{t-1}=p^A_{t-1}-\alpha-\beta p^B_{t-1}\) and \((\phi_A,\phi_B)\neq(0,0)\).

\(\phi\) = adjustment speed / loading · at least one asset must respond to the disequilibrium

This is the sentence that justifies the trade: if a pair is cointegrated, then yesterday's spread predicts today's returns, and somebody has to do the correcting. The signs tell you who: \(\phi_A<0\) means \(A\) falls back when the spread is too wide, \(\phi_B>0\) means \(B\) rises to meet it. If \(\phi_A=0\) and only \(\phi_B\neq 0\), then \(A\) is the price leader and \(B\) does all the adjusting — useful to know before deciding which leg is the “mispriced” one. Equivalently (Stock–Watson), \(n\) cointegrated series with rank \(r\) are driven by only \(n-r\) common stochastic trends: a cointegrated pair is one common trend plus one stationary residual.

Building the pair in practice

The Engle–Granger two-step is the workhorse for \(n=2\):

  1. Regress \(p^A_t\) on \(p^B_t\) (with a constant) over a formation window to obtain \(\hat\alpha,\hat\beta\) and residuals \(\hat e_t\). Because the regressors are \(I(1)\), \(\hat\beta\) is super-consistent: it converges at rate \(n\) rather than \(\sqrt n\), which is the one piece of good news in this literature.
  2. Test \(\hat e_t\) for a unit root with an augmented Dickey–Fuller regression \(\Delta\hat e_t=\rho\,\hat e_{t-1}+\sum_j\psi_j\Delta \hat e_{t-j}+\nu_t\) and reject \(H_0:\rho=0\) only against Engle–Granger critical values — not standard DF ones, because \(\hat e_t\) is a fitted residual and OLS has already worked to make it look stationary. For two variables with a constant, the 5% value is about \(-3.34\).

Four warnings that matter more than the algebra:

  • The regression is not symmetric. Regressing \(A\) on \(B\) and \(B\) on \(A\) gives hedge ratios that are not reciprocals; the difference is an attenuation factor \(R^2\). Total least squares, or the Johansen estimate, avoids having to nominate a dependent variable.
  • Power is low. Unit-root tests need long samples to distinguish a slow mean-reverter from a random walk. A half-life of six months in two years of data is essentially untestable.
  • Breaks masquerade as unit roots. A one-off level shift in a stationary spread makes ADF fail to reject (Perron). The converse also happens: a de-cointegrating drift looks fine in-sample right up to the point it doesn't.
  • \(\beta\) is not constant. Capital structure, index weights and betas move. Rolling windows or a Kalman filter with \(\beta_t\) as a random-walk state are the standard fixes, at the price of tracking your own noise.
For \(n>2\) assets use Johansen's procedure: estimate the VECM \(\Delta p_t = \Pi p_{t-1}+\dots\) by reduced-rank regression and read the cointegration rank off the trace or maximum-eigenvalue statistic, with \(\Pi=\phi\, b'\), \(\operatorname{rank}\Pi=r\). This handles baskets (a stock against a sector ETF, a bond against a curve), tests rank rather than assuming it, and treats all series symmetrically. Avellaneda and Lee's PCA-residual construction is the large-\(n\) cousin: regress on principal-component factors and trade the residual.

The spread as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

Cointegration says the spread is stationary; to size and time a trade you need a model of how it reverts. The continuous-time counterpart of the error-correction term is the OU process,

Model 5 — The spread
\begin{equation} de_t = -\kappa\,(e_t-\bar e)\,dt + \sigma_s\, dZ_t , \end{equation}
discretely, an AR(1): \(e_{t+\Delta}= \bar e+ e^{-\kappa\Delta}(e_t-\bar e)+\text{noise}\)

with the three numbers that decide whether the pair is worth trading:

Proposition 6 — Half-life, equilibrium scale, z-score
\begin{equation} t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{\kappa},\qquad e_\infty\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\bar e,\ \frac{\sigma_s^{2}}{2\kappa}\right),\qquad z_t=\frac{p^A_t-\hat\alpha-\hat\beta p^B_t}{\hat\sigma_e}. \end{equation}
from an AR(1) fit with coefficient \(\hat\rho\): \(\;t_{1/2}=-\ln 2/\ln\hat\rho\)

The half-life is the single most useful diagnostic. It sets the holding period, hence the number of independent bets per year, hence how much turnover cost you can absorb; and it must be short relative to your formation window or the estimate is meaningless. The trading rule is then a threshold policy on \(z\):

Definition 7 — Threshold rule and trade P&L

Enter \(\theta=-1\) (short the spread) when \(z_t>z_{\rm in}\), \(\theta=+1\) when \(z_t<-z_{\rm in}\); exit when \(|z_t|\le z_{\rm out}\); stop out when \(|z_t|\ge z_{\rm stop}\); flatten at the horizon. Per round trip, as a fraction of gross notional,

\begin{equation} \pi \;=\; \frac{\theta\,\bigl(e_{\rm exit}-e_{\rm entry}\bigr)}{1+\hat\beta}\;-\;2c , \end{equation}

and the return on equity at gross leverage \(L\) is \(R=L\sum_{\rm trades}\pi\).

c = one-way cost per unit notional (spread + fees + borrow), charged on both legs, in and out

There is an analytic literature on choosing \(z_{\rm in}\) optimally for an OU spread (Bertram, 2010; Elliott, van der Hoek and Malcolm, 2005), maximising expected return per unit time or a Sharpe-like objective. The qualitative answer is the one the simulator below reproduces: the optimum is interior, and it moves outward as costs rise.

A small stochastic model

One common stochastic trend \(f_t\), one stationary spread \(s_t\), and — crucially — one knob that breaks the cointegration:

\begin{align} df_t &= \mu\,dt + \sigma_f\,dW_t, &&\text{common trend, } I(1)\\ ds_t &= -\kappa\,s_t\,dt + \sigma_s\,dZ_t, &&\text{tether, } I(0)\\ du_t &= \sigma_u\,dB_t, &&\text{divergence, } I(1)\\ p^B_t &= p^B_0 + f_t, \qquad p^A_t = p^A_0 + \beta f_t + \underbrace{s_t+u_t+J\,\mathbf 1_{\{t\ge\tau\}}}_{e_t}. && \end{align}

When \(\sigma_u=0\) the pair is exactly cointegrated with vector \((1,-\beta)\), and in error-correction form \(\phi_A=-\kappa\), \(\phi_B=0\): all adjustment is done by \(A\). Switching on \(\sigma_u\) injects an idiosyncratic random walk into the spread, so \(e_t\) becomes \(I(1)\) and the relationship is no longer a relationship, only a slow one. That is the pairs-trading analogue of funding risk: the mechanism you were relying on quietly stops existing. Separately, a Poisson break with hazard \(h\) shifts the spread level permanently by \(J=4\,\sigma_s/\sqrt{2\kappa}\) — four equilibrium standard deviations, the merger that doesn't close, the index reconstitution, the profit warning.

The trader does not observe any of this. They see 252 days of prices (the formation window), run the Engle–Granger regression, form \(z_t\), and trade the next 126 days out of sample. Optionally, they refuse to trade the pair at all unless the ADF statistic rejects at 5%.

The distribution of outcomes

Two thousand paths on the same shock buffer. With \(\sigma_u=0\) the profile is a tidy hump of small gains: many small winners, a few losers where the spread simply failed to revert before the horizon. Turn up \(\sigma_u\) or \(h\) and the left tail thickens fast, because a spread that does not revert is not a position you exit — it is a position that keeps growing against you until the stop fires. The trade is short a small amount of convergence and long a large amount of regime stability.

Distribution of \(R=L\sum\pi\) over the 126-day trading window (% on equity)
Switch on the ADF filter and watch two things at once: the mean improves and the tail shortens, but the number of traded paths collapses. That trade-off — power against selectivity — is the real content of a cointegration test in a trading system. It is a gate, not an oracle: at \(\sigma_u>0\) a comfortable fraction of non-cointegrated pairs still pass, which is exactly how portfolios fill up with broken pairs.

Choosing the entry threshold

Where you set \(z_{\rm in}\) is the pairs-trading equivalent of choosing leverage. Enter too early and you trade constantly, capturing a few basis points per round trip and paying the full cost twice; enter too late and the spread rarely gets there, so capital sits idle and the few trades you do get are disproportionately the ones where something is genuinely wrong with the pair.

Drag the cost slider and the whole curve pivots: at \(c=0\) the optimum sits low, because every wiggle is worth harvesting; at 40–60 bp per leg the peak marches out past \(z=2\) and the achievable return shrinks toward zero. This is why stat-arb capacity is a function of execution quality rather than of ideas, and why the same signal can be profitable for one desk and unprofitable for another.

NOTE: maximising \(\mathbb E[R]\) ignores the shape of the distribution. A wider stop nearly always improves the mean in this model — it converts realised losses into unrealised ones and lets more of them revert — while making the tail much worse. Read the peak as a diagnostic, not a target, and look at the histogram beside it.

When the pair breaks

Every failure mode of a pairs trade is a version of the same sentence: the relationship you estimated was not the relationship that governed the future.

  • 1907–2005

    Royal Dutch / Shell. Two listings on a fixed 60:40 split of the same group cash flows — about as close to a mechanical cointegrating relation as public equity offers. The ratio nonetheless deviated from parity by tens of percent and stayed there for years at a time. Froot and Dabora used it as evidence that location of trade matters; LTCM used it as a position, and had to fund the deviation getting worse before it got better.

  • 1998

    LTCM. A book of relative-value convergence trades, individually sound and collectively one bet: that dislocations narrow. When Russia defaulted they widened together, correlations across supposedly independent spreads went to one, and margin calls forced liquidation into the very market the positions needed to be patient with.

  • Aug 2007

    The Quant Quake. Khandani and Lo documented several days in which long/short equity statistical arbitrage books lost heavily and then recovered most of it within a week. No relationship broke: the strategies were crowded, one large participant deleveraged, and everyone else's positions were the same positions. Cointegration says nothing about who else is in the trade.

  • Oct 2008

    Volkswagen / Porsche. A textbook cointegrated pair — ordinary and preference shares, holding company and subsidiary — annihilated by a disclosed option position that left a vanishing free float. The short leg went up fivefold in two days. Borrow recall and squeeze risk are not in the spread model, and they only ever hurt the leg you are short.

The common structure is worth stating plainly. A pairs trade is short a straddle on the relationship: bounded profit if the spread behaves, unbounded loss if it does not, and the premium is collected in small increments over many trades. Cointegration testing is how you try to make sure you are selling that option on something real. It never tells you what you are paid for the ones that are not.

A short checklist

QuestionWhy it decides the trade
Is there an economic reason these two prices are tied? Without one, the test is a data-mining artefact and the half-life is fiction.
What is the half-life? Sets holding period, number of independent bets, and cost tolerance. Longer than a quarter is usually untradable.
How stable is \(\hat\beta\) across sub-samples? An unstable hedge ratio means the residual contains factor exposure, not mispricing.
What does the spread do out of sample? In-sample stationarity is guaranteed by OLS; only the holdout is evidence.
What is the round-trip cost against the expected capture? Half of published pairs profitability disappears at realistic spreads and borrow fees.
What kills this pair? Merger, delisting, index change, capital raise, squeeze. Name it before you size it.
Who else owns it? Crowding turns a diversified book into a single position on liquidation days.
