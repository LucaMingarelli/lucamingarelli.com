A chronology of financial stability crises, market disruptions, and policy responses

A financial system is stable when it can keep providing its core services (credit, payments, liquidity, risk transfer) through shocks, rather than amplifying them. Crises are the moments when this fails. A loss that should have stayed local turns into fire sales, runs, and a sudden withdrawal of funding, and the damage spills over to the real economy.

Recurring vulnerabilities

The settings change, but crises tend to share a small number of mechanisms:

  • Leverage. Thin equity buffers turn modest price moves into solvency problems. Margin calls and haircuts then force deleveraging into falling markets.
  • Liquidity and maturity mismatch. Institutions that fund long-term or illiquid assets with short-term, runnable liabilities (deposits, repo, commercial paper, fund shares) are exposed to self-fulfilling runs.
  • Interconnectedness and common exposures. Counterparty chains and crowded trades spread distress through contagion and correlated selling.
  • The sovereign–bank nexus. Banks hold government debt and governments stand behind banks, so weakness on either side feeds the other.
  • Structural shifts. Intermediation migrates to non-banks: money funds, investment funds, hedge funds, pension schemes, and stablecoins. Liquidity risk moves with it, often outside the regulatory perimeter.

The policy toolkit

Responses have evolved with each episode. Central banks act as lender of last resort to solvent but illiquid banks and, increasingly, as market-maker of last resort to core markets such as sovereign bonds, repo and dollar funding via swap lines. Supervisors rely on microprudential rules (capital, liquidity, stress tests) and, since 2008, on macroprudential tools aimed at the system as a whole (countercyclical buffers, borrower-based measures, surcharges for systemic institutions). Resolution regimes and bail-in aim to let large banks fail without taxpayer bailouts. Many of the entries below record when and why each of these tools was first used.

Mar – Jul 2026

Escalating Middle-East conflict and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz#

Geopolitics / Trade & Energy Markets

In March 2026, U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iranian targets escalated into open war. Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz from 4 March. The Strait carries roughly a fifth of global oil supply and a large share of world LNG trade, and the International Energy Agency called the shock the "greatest global energy security challenge in history". Brent crude briefly traded above $120 and European gas prices nearly doubled. Equities and sovereign bonds sold off together as inflation fears overrode the usual safe-haven role of government debt. The shock proved temporary. As tensions eased into the summer, risk premia unwound: Brent was back near $72 by early July, and the S&P 500 had recovered more than 17% from its March low.

Details
Trigger and energy shock

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz on 4 March was the epicentre of the disruption. QatarEnergy declared force majeure on its exports. Output from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, and the UAE was stranded, deepening the global supply squeeze. Brent rose above $120 intraday in the initial panic and reached a peak close of $118.35 on 31 March. Tanker freight rates spiked as vessels dropped anchor or rerouted away from the conflict zone.

Market transmission
  • Equities. Global stock markets sold off as the conflict escalated. The S&P 500 fell throughout March and bottomed at 6,316.91 on 30 March.
  • Tech as a relative safe haven. Large U.S. technology firms, which are less sensitive to energy costs, held up well and limited losses in the broader U.S. index.
  • Sovereign bonds. U.S. Treasuries and other sovereign bonds sold off. Markets priced out expected rate cuts and began to price possible further hikes to counter stagflation.
Real-economy spillovers
  • Gulf states (GCC). GCC states import the large majority of their food, much of it through the Strait, and the blockade caused an acute grocery-supply emergency. Retailers airlifted staples, adding surcharges of 40–120%. Iranian strikes on desalination plants in Kuwait and Qatar raised fears of a drinking-water crisis.
  • Europe. The halt in Qatari LNG came after a winter that had left gas storage historically low. Dutch TTF prices nearly doubled, exceeding €60/MWh by mid-March. Chemical and steel producers in the EU and UK imposed surcharges of around 30%, reviving fears of lasting deindustrialisation.
  • Asia. Import-dependent economies such as Vietnam, the Philippines and Bangladesh faced fuel shortages and panic buying, leading to states of emergency and transport strikes.
Policy response

Central banks shifted to a defensive, inflation-focused stance and pushed back expected easing. (To be completed: specific central-bank, fiscal and IEA stock-release measures.)

Recovery

As in earlier oil shocks, the most extreme price moves were short-lived. By 1 July, Brent had fallen to $71.57, fully reversing the war-driven spike. The S&P 500 rose 17.3% from its March low to close at 7,411.98 on 24 July.

Financial-stability reading
  • Stocks and bonds fell together. As in 2022, a supply-driven inflation shock turned the stock–bond correlation positive. This weakened the diversification that balanced and risk-parity portfolios rely on.
  • Commodity-derivative margins. Sharp moves in energy prices tend to raise initial and variation margin for producers, utilities and commodity traders. This is the liquidity channel that strained European energy firms in 2022.
  • Rate repricing. A sudden shift in the expected policy path affects interest-rate-sensitive balance sheets. Leveraged holders of duration are the most exposed.
  • Temporary shock. The fast reversal fits the pattern of geopolitical supply shocks: severe price effects, but limited lasting damage to the financial system when the disruption is short.

Apr – Jun 2025

"Liberation Day" tariffs and the April 2025 market crash#

Geopolitics / Trade & Energy Markets Sovereign

On 2 April 2025 the U.S. administration announced sweeping "reciprocal" tariffs that went far beyond what markets expected: a 10% baseline on nearly all imports, plus much higher country-specific rates, including 34% on China, 20% on the EU and 24% on Japan. U.S. equities lost about $6.6 trillion in two sessions, the largest two-day loss on record, and the sell-off spread worldwide. The episode was unusual for a risk-off shock. Instead of rallying, U.S. Treasuries sold off and the dollar weakened, which raised questions about the safe-haven status of U.S. assets. Leveraged bond positions came under strain. On 9 April the administration announced a 90-day pause on most of the new tariffs, setting off one of the largest one-day rallies in history. By late June, the S&P 500 and Nasdaq were at new record highs.

Details
Trigger

The administration dubbed 2 April "Liberation Day". The tariffs aimed to penalise the countries with the largest bilateral trade surpluses with the U.S. and to bring manufacturing back home. The package included:

  • a flat 10% baseline tariff on nearly all imports;
  • 34% on China, which escalated through tit-for-tat retaliation to a combined U.S. rate of 145% by mid-April, against Chinese retaliation of 125%;
  • 20% on the European Union and 24% on Japan.

The measures were abrupt and hit close allies and export-dependent economies. Investors quickly priced the risk of retaliation and a prolonged global trade war.

Global contagion and commodities
  • Asia. Japan's Nikkei 225 fell almost 8% on 7 April, triggering circuit breakers in futures trading. South Korea's KOSPI fell more than 5%.
  • Europe. The STOXX 600 fell more than 8% in a single week, its worst week since 2020. The FTSE 100 dropped almost 5% in a day, its largest daily fall since the March 2020 "dash for cash".
  • Commodities. On expectations of a global slowdown, crude oil fell more than 7%, and WTI briefly traded below $60 per barrel for the first time since 2021.
Treasury market and leverage

This part of the episode mattered most for financial stability. In a typical global sell-off, investors buy U.S. Treasuries. In the week of 7–11 April, Treasury yields instead rose sharply, especially at long maturities, while equities were also falling. Market commentary and official reports pointed to forced deleveraging of crowded leveraged positions, particularly swap-spread trades and, possibly, the Treasury cash–futures basis trade (see the basis-trade page). They also noted reduced dealer capacity to absorb sales. The bond-market stress was widely cited as one reason for the 9 April pause. The dollar anomaly

The dollar weakened alongside rising Treasury yields, an unusual combination in a risk-off episode. This reflected concern about unpredictable, weaponised trade policy and about the long-run safe-haven and reserve status of U.S. assets. The dollar fell about 6.5% against the euro in the weeks after the announcement. The yen and Swiss franc appreciated as alternative safe havens. Gold set successive records, above $3,100/oz in early April and higher still later in the month.

Policy response

The main response came from trade policy itself: the 90-day pause on 9 April, followed by further walk-backs and negotiations. Central banks did not need to intervene in markets.   • The safe-haven assumption was tested. Treasuries and the dollar fell together with equities. That challenges a central assumption in global risk management and collateral frameworks.
  • Leverage in the core bond market. As in March 2020, stress in the Treasury market drew attention to hedge-fund relative-value leverage and to dealers' limited capacity to intermediate.
  • Market discipline on policy. The speed of the reversal shows bond markets acting as a constraint on economic policy, echoing the UK gilt crisis of 2022.
  • Lasting effects. Prices recovered quickly. Questions about dollar volatility and the stability of trade relations remained.

Mar – May 2023

US regional bank failures and the collapse of Credit Suisse#

Banking Policy / Regulation

The fastest-moving banking crisis since 2008. Mid-sized US banks had invested a surge of pandemic-era deposits in long-dated Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities. When the Federal Reserve raised rates in 2022–23, these portfolios suffered large unrealised losses. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank's mostly uninsured, tech-sector depositors ran, withdrawing $42bn in a single day, and the bank failed within 48 hours. Signature Bank followed, and First Republic was seized and sold to JPMorgan Chase on 1 May. US authorities guaranteed all deposits at the failed banks and created the Bank Term Funding Program, which lent against bonds at par. The loss of confidence then spread to Europe's weakest global bank. On 19 March, Swiss authorities arranged a weekend takeover of Credit Suisse by UBS for CHF 3bn. They backed the deal with extraordinary liquidity support and wrote off CHF 16bn of AT1 bonds while shareholders received some value, a decision that shook global bank capital markets.

Details
1 · The US regional banking crisis#
Setup: deposit surge and duration risk

Fiscal stimulus, ultra-low rates, and a tech funding boom produced a flood of deposits into regional banks during the pandemic. SVB's deposits roughly tripled, from about $62bn at end-2019 to about $189bn at end-2021. Loan demand did not keep pace, so banks invested the cash in long-dated US Treasuries and agency MBS. These assets carried little credit risk but a great deal of interest-rate (duration) risk.

When the Fed raised rates sharply from March 2022, bond prices fell and the portfolios showed large unrealised losses. Securities classified as held to maturity did not have to be marked to market in regulatory capital. The losses were harmless only as long as the bank never had to sell. A run would force sales and turn the paper losses into real ones.

The failure of Silicon Valley Bank

SVB's funding was unusually fragile. Its depositors were start-ups and venture-capital firms, a concentrated and closely networked client base, and over 90% of deposits exceeded the $250,000 FDIC insurance limit. As start-ups burned through cash, deposits had been flowing out since 2022.

Policy response
  • Blanket deposit guarantee. All deposits at SVB and Signature were protected, so uninsured depositors lost nothing. Shareholders and unsecured creditors were not protected. The cost fell on the Deposit Insurance Fund, recovered through a special assessment on large banks.
  • Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP). The facility offered loans of up to one year against Treasuries and agency securities valued at par rather than market value. This removed the need for fire sales and effectively neutralised the unrealised losses for liquidity purposes. New lending under the BTFP stopped in March 2024.
  • Discount window. Borrowing reached a record of about $153bn in the week of 15 March, exceeding the 2008 peak.
  • Supervisory review. The Fed's own review (the "Barr report", April 2023) acknowledged supervisory failures. It also pointed to the 2019 "tailoring" that had eased requirements for banks of SVB's size, including exemption from the liquidity coverage ratio and the option to exclude unrealised losses on securities from regulatory capital.
2 · The demise of Credit Suisse#
Background: a long loss of confidence

The US failures were driven by interest-rate risk and fragile funding. Credit Suisse's fall was different. It was the culmination of years of scandals, risk-management failures, and repeated losses, most notably the Archegos and Greensill episodes of 2021. A wave of client withdrawals in October 2022 left the bank highly exposed to any new shock. Net asset outflows totalled about CHF 171bn between Q4 2022 and Q1 2023.

The final blow

The US turmoil quickly crossed the Atlantic and targeted the weakest global bank in Europe. On 15 March, Credit Suisse's largest shareholder, the Saudi National Bank, ruled out providing more capital, and the share price and bond prices collapsed. Late on 15 March, Credit Suisse announced it would borrow up to CHF 50bn from the Swiss National Bank. This failed to halt the outflows or the slide in its shares.

The weekend rescue

Swiss authorities (the Federal Council, the SNB, and FINMA) feared that the disorderly failure of a global systemically important bank (G-SIB) would spread worldwide. They chose an emergency merger over the bank's resolution plan. On Sunday 19 March 2023, UBS agreed to acquire Credit Suisse in an all-stock deal worth about CHF 3bn (about $3.2bn). Shareholders did not get a vote, as the deal relied on emergency law. To secure it, the state provided:

  • up to CHF 100bn of liquidity assistance from the SNB;
  • a further CHF 100bn public liquidity backstop, an SNB loan carrying a federal default guarantee;
  • a CHF 9bn federal loss guarantee on a designated portfolio of hard-to-value assets, covering losses after UBS had absorbed the first CHF 5bn. UBS ended this agreement in August 2023 without using it.
The AT1 write-down

The most contested part of the rescue was FINMA's order to write down Credit Suisse's Additional Tier 1 (AT1) bonds, about CHF 16bn, to zero. AT1s are contingent-convertible instruments designed to absorb losses while a bank is still operating. Their prospectuses did allow a full write-down on a "viability event" or when extraordinary government support is provided. However, the usual creditor hierarchy puts AT1 holders ahead of shareholders, and here the shareholders still received about CHF 3bn in UBS shares while AT1 holders lost everything.

The AT1 market had last been seriously tested in early 2016, over doubts about Deutsche Bank's coupon payments. The decision caused a sharp sell-off in AT1 bonds worldwide. On 20 March, the ECB, the Single Resolution Board, and the EBA issued a joint statement, and the Bank of England a separate one, both confirming that in their jurisdictions equity would absorb losses before AT1. Bondholders brought numerous legal challenges. In 2025 the Swiss Federal Administrative Court was reported to have found that the write-down lacked a sufficient legal basis, a ruling FINMA has appealed. (verify status)

Financial-stability reading
  • Runs are faster in the digital age. Mobile banking and social media compressed a bank run from weeks to hours. This challenges liquidity rules calibrated on 30-day outflow assumptions.
  • Uninsured deposits behave like wholesale funding. A concentrated, networked base of uninsured deposits is highly runnable. The blanket guarantee reopened the debate on the scope of deposit insurance.
  • Interest-rate risk on the banking book. Held-to-maturity accounting can hide economic insolvency until liquidity needs force sales. Liquidity and solvency risk are tightly linked.
  • "Mid-sized" banks can be systemic. Authorities treated banks that were not designated as systemic as if they were, which raised questions about the 2019 tailoring rules.
  • Resolution frameworks were tested. The first failure of a G-SIB since the post-2008 reforms was handled by a state-backed merger rather than bail-in resolution. This raised doubts about whether the framework could be used in practice and led to reviews in Switzerland and by the Basel Committee and FSB (2023).

Sep – Oct 2022

UK "mini-budget", the gilt market crisis and leveraged LDI funds#

Sovereign Markets Non-bank Policy / Regulation

On 23 September 2022 the UK government announced a "mini-budget" with about £45bn of unfunded tax cuts, at a time of high inflation and rising interest rates. Gilts sold off at a speed not seen in modern times: 30-year yields rose by more than 160bp in a matter of days, and sterling fell to a record low. The fiscal shock was then amplified by leveraged liability-driven investment (LDI) funds used by defined-benefit pension schemes. Rising yields triggered collateral calls that the funds could meet only by selling long-dated gilts, which pushed yields higher still in a self-reinforcing spiral. On 28 September the Bank of England stepped in as market-maker of last resort with temporary, targeted purchases of long-dated gilts. Combined with the reversal of the fiscal package, this restored order by mid-October. The episode became a reference case for leverage and liquidity risk in non-banks.

Details
Trigger: the "mini-budget"

The newly appointed Chancellor, Kwasi Kwarteng, announced the package on 23 September. It came without an independent forecast from the Office for Budget Responsibility, while the Bank of England was raising rates to fight double-digit inflation. Investors doubted the government's commitment to fiscal discipline and expected heavy additional borrowing. Gilts and sterling sold off together, a pattern more typical of emerging markets than of a major advanced economy.

The vulnerability: leveraged LDI

UK defined-benefit (DB) pension schemes promise payments linked to long-term interest rates and inflation. To hedge their liabilities, many use LDI strategies, held through pooled funds shared by many smaller schemes or through segregated mandates for larger ones.

  • Leverage. Instead of buying gilts outright with cash, LDI funds use gilt repo and interest-rate swaps to gain more exposure to long-dated gilts than their capital alone would allow. This leaves the rest of the scheme's assets free to invest in higher-return assets such as equities.
  • Collateral. These positions require collateral. When yields rise, the value of the gilts and swaps falls, and counterparties (mostly banks) make margin and collateral calls that must be met in cash or high-quality assets within days.
  • Buffers. Before the crisis, LDI funds typically held buffers sized for yield rises of around 100–150bp. That is ample by historical standards, but far less than the moves of late September.

The schemes themselves were not insolvent. Higher yields reduce the value of pension liabilities, and most schemes' funding positions improved. The problem was liquidity: the cash needed to keep the hedges in place had to be found immediately.

A simple approximation shows why the buffers were exhausted so quickly. For an LDI fund with leverage \(L\) (gilt exposure ÷ fund NAV) holding assets of modified duration \(D\), a parallel yield rise \(\Delta y\) changes NAV by roughly

$$ \frac{\Delta \mathrm{NAV}}{\mathrm{NAV}} \;\approx\; -\,L \cdot D \cdot \Delta y . $$

With \(L = 3\), \(D = 20\), and \(\Delta y = 1\%\), the fund loses about 60% of its NAV. A move of 1.5% would wipe it out completely unless new cash arrives.

The fire-sale spiral

Pooled funds in particular could not raise cash from hundreds of client schemes fast enough. Many had to sell the assets they held, mainly long-dated and index-linked gilts, into a falling market:

  1. Yields rise and the value of LDI positions falls.
  2. Counterparties call for more collateral.
  3. Funds sell long-dated gilts to raise cash.
  4. Forced sales overwhelm dealers' capacity, and yields rise further.

The forced selling is estimated to have accounted for a large part of the fall in long-dated gilt prices, possibly around half at the peak. (to source) Without intervention, a large share of LDI funds would have lost their hedges, and the long end of the gilt market risked ceasing to function.

Policy response
  • Market-maker of last resort. The purchases were explicitly a financial-stability operation, separate from monetary policy. They were time-limited, targeted at the dysfunctional segment, and designed to buy time for LDI funds to rebuild resilience, not to lower yields permanently. The Bank sold the gilts back by January 2023 at a profit.
  • Fiscal correction. Restoring confidence ultimately depended on reversing the package and on a new Chancellor with a credible fiscal plan.
  • Higher resilience standards. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee and The Pensions Regulator (2023) set a minimum steady-state resilience of about 250bp of yield rise. The Central Bank of Ireland and Luxembourg's CSSF, where most pooled LDI funds are domiciled, later formalised a 300bp requirement for sterling LDI funds. In practice most funds now hold buffers of about 300–400bp.
  • New central-bank facility. The episode led the Bank of England to design a standing repo facility through which eligible non-banks, including LDI funds, pension schemes, and insurers, can borrow against gilts in stress: the Contingent NBFI Repo Facility. (verify dates)
Financial-stability reading
  • Hedging can create liquidity risk. Strategies that reduce long-term (solvency) risk can create acute short-term liquidity needs. The same mechanics appeared earlier in 2022 in energy-derivative margins.
  • Stress outside the banking system. The amplifier was leverage in non-banks that sat largely outside macroprudential oversight. It was spread across funds domiciled in Ireland and Luxembourg and supervised on a fund-by-fund basis, so no one saw the combined fire-sale risk.
  • Stress scenarios were too mild. Buffers calibrated on historical yield moves were overtaken by a move that broke past records. The calibration did not allow for the fact that the funds' own forced sales would push yields higher, the same self-reinforcing dynamic seen in the 2018 short-volatility unwind.
  • A template for intervention. The operation established the principle of temporary, targeted, financial-stability purchases that are kept separate from monetary policy, and that can run even while the central bank is tightening.
  • Bond markets discipline fiscal policy. Even a reserve-currency advanced economy can face a sharp loss of investor confidence when fiscal credibility is in doubt. Compare the April 2025 Treasury sell-off, and contrast the 2016 Brexit vote, when sterling fell but gilts rallied.

Feb – Jun 2022

Russian invasion of Ukraine: sanctions, commodity shock, and market dysfunction#

Geopolitics / Trade & Energy Markets Banking Policy / Regulation

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 caused a sharp shock to global markets. Unlike 2008 or March 2020, it did not come from a hidden flaw inside the financial system. It came from war, from sanctions of unprecedented scope, and from disruption to commodity supply. Western governments froze about $300bn of the Bank of Russia's foreign reserves and cut major Russian banks off from SWIFT. Within days, Sberbank's European subsidiary failed. Energy prices hit records: Brent briefly approached $140 and European gas exceeded €300/MWh. On the London Metal Exchange, a short squeeze pushed nickel above $100,000 a tonne, and the exchange suspended trading and cancelled trades. In Russia, the ruble crashed, the central bank doubled its policy rate to 20% and imposed capital controls, and the stock market closed for almost a month. In Europe, margin calls on energy derivatives became a liquidity problem for utilities. The energy shock fed inflation worldwide and became a key factor in the rapid monetary tightening of 2022–23.

Details
Financial sanctions

Within days of the invasion, the US, EU, UK, and their allies adopted coordinated financial sanctions to isolate the Russian economy. Two measures were without precedent for a G20 economy:

  • Freezing central-bank reserves. From 26–28 February, transactions with the Bank of Russia were prohibited. This immobilised about $300bn, roughly half of its reserves, held in Western jurisdictions, and sharply limited its ability to defend the ruble by selling dollars and euros.
  • SWIFT disconnection. On 2 March the EU removed seven banks from SWIFT: VTB, Otkritie, Novikombank, Promsvyazbank, Bank Rossiya, Sovcombank and VEB. Sberbank, the largest, was disconnected in June 2022. Cut off from SWIFT messaging, these banks could barely process cross-border payments.
A bank failure inside the euro area: Sberbank Europe

The first bank casualty was inside the EU. Sberbank Europe AG, the Vienna-based subsidiary of Russia's largest state bank, had operations in several central and eastern European countries. After the invasion it suffered a deposit run driven by reputational damage and fear of sanctions.

  1. 27–28 Feb 2022
    Failing or likely to failThe ECB declares Sberbank Europe AG and its Croatian and Slovenian subsidiaries failing or likely to fail because of a sharp deterioration in liquidity. The Single Resolution Board imposes a moratorium on payments.
  2. 1 Mar
    ResolutionThe SRB sells Sberbank Croatia to Hrvatska Poštanska Banka and Sberbank Slovenia to NLB. It decides that resolving the Austrian parent is not in the public interest, and the parent enters national insolvency proceedings.

Western banks with large Russian operations also sold off sharply. Société Générale sold its Russian subsidiary Rosbank to Interros in spring 2022 and took a loss of about €3bn. Raiffeisen Bank International and UniCredit kept their Russian operations and reduced them gradually under pressure from supervisors and governments.

Commodities and markets
  • Equities. Global stocks fell. European markets fell most, given their proximity to the conflict, their dependence on Russian energy, and banks' exposures to the region.
  • Energy. Brent reached about $139 intraday on 7 March, its highest since 2008. Dutch TTF gas reached a record of about €345/MWh intraday on the same day, and stayed extremely high and volatile through the summer as Russian pipeline flows were cut.
  • Food and fertiliser. Russia and Ukraine are major exporters of wheat, maize, sunflower oil, and fertiliser, and prices for all of them rose sharply, hitting import-dependent emerging economies hardest.
The LME nickel squeeze#

Russia is a leading producer of high-grade nickel. Fears of lost supply caused a short squeeze against large short positions, most notably those held by China's Tsingshan Holding Group, mostly through over-the-counter positions with banks. Nickel rose about 250% in two days and traded above $100,000 a tonne on the morning of 8 March. Many clearing members faced variation-margin calls they could not meet.

The LME suspended nickel trading, which stayed shut for about a week, and cancelled about $3.9bn of trades made that morning. It argued this was necessary to prevent a chain of clearing-member defaults. The cancellation was highly controversial because it reversed already-executed trades. Hedge funds challenged it in court, and the High Court upheld the LME's decision in 2023. The UK Financial Conduct Authority later fined the LME for weaknesses in its systems and controls during the episode. (verify: about £9.2m, 2025)

Energy derivatives and utilities' liquidity#

European energy producers and utilities hedge future output by selling forward on exchanges, where positions are centrally cleared and margined. When prices rise sharply, these short hedges lose value on paper, and the firms face large variation-margin and higher initial-margin calls. They are solvent, because they own the physical energy, but they suddenly need cash. Throughout 2022 this created severe liquidity strain, with the same mechanics as the LDI crisis later that year.

  • Several governments set up emergency liquidity lines or guarantees for energy companies, including Finland and Sweden in September 2022.
  • Germany's Uniper, the largest importer of Russian gas, had to buy replacement gas at spot prices after Russian supplies stopped. It was rescued and then nationalised, at a cost of tens of billions of euros. (figures to verify)
  • EU authorities temporarily widened the collateral eligible at central counterparties to ease the pressure on non-financial firms.
Russia's domestic market
  • Ruble. The ruble traded at about 75–80 to the dollar before the invasion. It fell beyond 130 in offshore trading in early March as households queued at ATMs for foreign currency. After capital controls and the surrender requirement took effect, it rebounded to about 55–60 by June, stronger than before the war.
  • Bank of Russia response. On 28 February, the central bank raised its key rate from 9.5% to 20%. It imposed capital controls, including a ban on foreigners selling Russian securities, and required exporters to convert 80% of their foreign-currency revenue into rubles.
  • Stock market. The MOEX index fell about 33% on 24 February. Share trading was suspended from 28 February and resumed only partially on 24 March, with short-selling banned and foreign investors unable to sell.
  • Sovereign default. In June 2022, sanctions prevented foreign-currency coupon payments from reaching investors, and the grace period expired. This was Russia's first foreign-currency default since 1918, caused by sanctions rather than by an inability to pay.
Macroeconomic spillovers

The war's most lasting financial effect came through inflation. Central banks were already facing post-pandemic supply-chain inflation. Higher prices for fuel, fertiliser, and food pushed inflation higher and kept it elevated for longer. The Bank of England had begun raising rates in December 2021 and the Federal Reserve did so in March 2022, as planned before the invasion. The ECB followed in July 2022. All three tightened faster and further than expected through 2022–23. This rapid rise in rates was a key factor in ending the low-rate era that began after 2008, and in creating the interest-rate losses behind the 2023 bank failures.

Financial-stability reading
  • Finance as a weapon. Freezing a G20 central bank's reserves showed that reserves held abroad can be immobilised. This has fed reserve diversification, including into gold, and the debate on alternatives to dollar- and euro-based payment systems.
  • Margin calls as a liquidity channel. Central clearing reduces counterparty risk but turns price shocks into sudden cash demands. Energy firms, like LDI funds later, were solvent but short of liquidity.
  • Market infrastructure can fail under stress. The LME episode exposed weak oversight of large OTC positions and of exchange governance. Cancelling trades protected the clearing house but damaged confidence in the market.
  • Exposure through foreign subsidiaries. Sberbank Europe showed how quickly a subsidiary with a sanctioned parent can lose its funding. It was also an early test of the EU resolution framework for a run driven by geopolitics rather than by losses.
  • From energy shock to inflation to rates. The shock to the real economy turned into a financial-stability risk with a lag, through higher interest rates and the duration losses they caused.

Mar 2021

Collapse of Archegos Capital Management#

Non-bank Markets Banking

In the last week of March 2021, Archegos Capital Management, the family office of former hedge-fund manager Bill Hwang, defaulted on margin calls and lost capital of around $36bn within days. Archegos had built highly concentrated positions in a handful of US and Chinese stocks, worth about $160bn gross, using total return swaps with several prime brokers at once. Because of the swaps and its family-office status, no regulator, and none of the banks, could see the full size of its positions. When ViacomCBS shares fell after a stock offering, the margin calls could not be met. The banks then raced each other to sell the collateral. Those that sold first, mainly Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, lost little. Credit Suisse lost about $5.5bn and Nomura about $2.9bn. The episode was not systemic, but it showed how non-bank leverage can hide inside bank balance sheets, and it accelerated the decline of Credit Suisse.

Details
Setup: a family office with hidden leverage

In 2012 Hwang's hedge fund, Tiger Asia, settled SEC insider-trading charges, and he was barred from managing outside money. He turned his own wealth into Archegos and registered it as a family office. Because it managed only family money, it was exempt from most of the reporting that hedge funds must file, such as Form PF. The strategy rested on two features:

  • Extreme concentration. Archegos held very large positions in a few names, notably ViacomCBS, Discovery, Baidu, Tencent Music, GSX Techedu, Vipshop, and iQIYI. Its own buying helped drive some of these stocks sharply higher; ViacomCBS roughly tripled in a few months.
  • Total return swaps (TRS). Rather than buying shares, Archegos entered swaps. The prime broker bought and held the shares as a hedge, paid Archegos the stock's total return, and charged a financing spread in exchange. Archegos posted only a fraction of the position's value as margin.

Cash-settled swaps did not count toward the 5% beneficial-ownership disclosure threshold, and they do not appear in 13F filings. Archegos spread its positions across Credit Suisse, Nomura, Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, UBS, MUFG, Mizuho, and others, so each bank saw only its own slice. Together, several banks held large stakes in the same stocks on Archegos's behalf. According to US prosecutors, Archegos's capital grew from about $1.5bn in early 2020 to about $36bn, against gross exposure of about $160bn, overall leverage of roughly 4–5×, and higher on individual positions.

Trigger and collapse
  1. 22–23 Mar 2021
    ViacomCBS offeringViacomCBS announces and prices a stock offering of about $3bn to fund its streaming business. Investors see it as dilutive, and the shares start to fall. Chinese tech holdings fall at the same time.
  2. 24 Mar
    Equity buffer goneViacomCBS falls 23% in a day. With its leverage, Archegos's margin is exhausted, and it tells its prime brokers it cannot meet the margin calls.
  3. 25 Mar
    Failed standstillThe prime brokers meet Archegos to discuss a coordinated, orderly unwind. No agreement is reached. Morgan Stanley begins selling blocks that evening.
  4. 26 Mar
    Fire saleGoldman Sachs sells about $10bn of stock in block trades, and other banks follow. ViacomCBS closes near $48, about half its level of a week earlier. Discovery and the Chinese ADRs also fall sharply.
  5. 29 Mar
    Losses disclosedCredit Suisse and Nomura warn of significant losses.
A race for the exit

The unwind was a collective-action problem. Every bank would have done better under a coordinated sale, but each had an incentive to sell first. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley sold early and in size, and their contracts allowed faster close-out, so they lost little. Credit Suisse and Nomura held back. By the time they sold, prices had fallen much further, and their collateral no longer covered their exposure. Across the affected stocks, more than $30bn of market value was lost in a few days. Aftermath
  • Credit Suisse. The independent review by Paul, Weiss (July 2021) found that the bank had repeatedly ignored limit breaches and had a "lackadaisical" attitude to risk. In 2023 the Fed fined CS $269m and the UK Prudential Regulation Authority fined it £87m. Together with Greensill, Archegos was a key step toward the collapse of 2023.

  • Regulation. In 2023 the SEC updated its beneficial-ownership rules, including guidance on cash-settled swaps, and started security-based swap reporting. In 2024 the Basel Committee issued guidelines on banks' counterparty credit risk management with non-banks, citing Archegos. (verify details)
Financial-stability reading
  • Non-bank leverage on bank balance sheets. The risk sat in an unregulated non-bank, but banks provided the leverage and took the losses. Counterparty credit risk is the channel through which non-bank stress reaches the banking system.
  • Aggregate exposure was invisible. Each bank managed its own exposure but did not know the total. Gaps in disclosure (family-office exemption, swap reporting) meant no one could see crowding in the same stocks.
  • Margin models and concentration. Margins that do not account for concentration and liquidation cost are too low for very large, illiquid positions. The fall in prices from forced selling becomes part of the loss.
  • Fire sales between creditors. Selling first is rational for each creditor, but the race destroys collateral value for all, the same dynamic as a bank run.
  • Contained, not systemic. Strong bank capital absorbed about $10bn of losses without wider contagion. The damage to risk culture and confidence at one bank, though, later fed into a G-SIB failure.

Feb – Apr 2020

COVID-19 and the global "dash for cash"#

Markets Non-bank Sovereign Policy / Regulation

As the COVID-19 pandemic and lockdowns spread in March 2020, the usual flight to safety turned into a flight to liquidity. Investors and firms around the world sold almost everything, including US Treasuries, the world's main safe asset, to obtain dollar cash. In mid-March Treasury yields rose while equities were collapsing, trading costs jumped, and the deepest bond market in the world struggled to function. The selling came mostly from outside the banking system: foreign official holders, bond and money market funds facing redemptions, and leveraged hedge funds unwinding the cash–futures basis trade. Dealers, constrained by their balance sheets, could not absorb the flows. Firms drew down about $240bn of bank credit lines, commercial paper markets froze, and global dollar funding tightened. The Federal Reserve responded on an unprecedented scale. It cut rates to zero, bought Treasuries at up to about $75bn a day, reopened and extended its 2008 facilities, announced direct purchases of corporate bonds, and expanded dollar swap lines and a new repo facility for foreign central banks. Markets stabilised after 23 March.

Details
From flight to safety to flight to liquidity

From mid-February to early March, markets behaved as expected in a crisis. Equities fell, Treasuries rallied, and the 10-year yield fell to a record low of about 0.5% on 9 March. In the second week of March the pattern reversed. As the economic consequences of lockdowns became clear, the demand for cash overtook the demand for safe assets. The 10-year yield rose to about 1.2% by 18 March while stocks were still falling. Bid-ask spreads widened sharply, especially for older ("off-the-run") Treasuries, and market depth collapsed. The VIX closed at a record 82.7 on 16 March.

Mechanism: why Treasuries were sold
  • Foreign official sellers. Central banks and other official institutions in emerging and oil-exporting economies needed dollars to support their currencies as capital fled and oil prices fell. They sold Treasuries from their reserves. (size to verify: ≈ $100bn+ in March)
  • Fund redemptions. Bond mutual funds and money market funds faced large outflows. Funds sold their most liquid holdings, mainly Treasuries, to meet redemptions, rather than less liquid corporate bonds. Prime institutional money market funds suffered a run.
  • Basis-trade unwind. In 2019–20 hedge funds had built large (a build-up already visible in the September 2019 repo spike), highly leveraged positions buying cash Treasuries with repo funding and selling futures against them (see the basis-trade page). When volatility spiked and the cash–futures spread moved against them, higher margins and repo haircuts forced them to sell cash Treasuries into a market already short of buyers.
  • Dealer balance-sheet limits. Primary dealers normally absorb selling into inventory. In March 2020 the volume of sales exceeded their capacity. Post-2008 rules, notably the supplementary leverage ratio, make holding Treasuries balance-sheet-intensive, so dealers widened prices instead of buying. The market had grown much faster than dealers' capacity to intermediate it.
  • Pre-emptive selling. Research on the episode finds that investors who did not need cash immediately also sold, fearing that market functioning would deteriorate further. This made the sell-off self-reinforcing until the Fed stepped in.
Corporate and short-term funding markets
  • Credit-line drawdowns. With revenues falling sharply, firms drew on committed bank credit lines to build cash. Drawdowns in a few weeks totalled roughly $240bn (NBER research). The lines worked as designed, but they used up bank balance sheets and reduced banks' willingness to make markets.
  • Commercial paper. Issuance markets seized up, even for highly rated firms, and CP rates jumped. Money market funds, the main buyers of CP, were themselves facing outflows.
  • Credit and other markets. Corporate bond, municipal bond, and mortgage-backed securities markets became highly illiquid. Corporate bond ETFs traded at large discounts to their net asset value. In the UK, open-ended property funds suspended dealing, as they had after the 2016 Brexit vote.
Global dollar shortage

Foreign banks and firms with short-term dollar funding needs found it harder to roll over. The cost of borrowing dollars through FX swaps (the cross-currency basis) rose sharply. Emerging markets saw record portfolio outflows and currency depreciation. The UK gilt market went through a similar dash for cash, which the Bank of England answered with £200bn of purchases announced on 19 March.

Financial-stability reading
  • Even safe assets can become illiquid. The Treasury market's liquidity depends on dealers' capacity to intermediate. When everyone sells at once, safety does not guarantee liquidity. Smaller warnings had come from the 2015 Bund tantrum and the October 2014 Treasury flash rally.
  • Stress came from non-banks. Banks were well capitalised and did not fail. The amplifiers were liquidity mismatch in funds, leverage in hedge funds, and reserve managers' dollar needs.
  • The Fed as global market-maker of last resort. The Fed backstopped not only banks but also core markets, non-financial firms and foreign central banks. That set a precedent for interventions such as the Bank of England's 2022 gilt operation, and raised moral-hazard concerns.
  • Reform agenda. It also led to the SEC's 2023 money market fund reform and the FSB's work on open-ended fund liquidity and margin practices.
  • Not resolved. The same vulnerabilities, leveraged relative-value trades and limited dealer capacity, came back into focus in the April 2025 Treasury sell-off.

Sep 2019

The US repo rate spike#

Markets Banking Policy / Regulation

On 16–17 September 2019, overnight rates for borrowing cash against US Treasuries jumped from about 2% to as high as 10% intraday, and the effective federal funds rate rose above the Fed's target range. This was not a credit or solvency crisis like 2008, but a failure of market plumbing. Two routine cash drains, corporate tax payments and the settlement of new Treasury debt, hit a system with far fewer spare reserves than the Fed had assumed. After years of balance-sheet reduction, reserves had fallen to about $1.4tn. They were concentrated in a few large banks, which preferred to hold them to meet liquidity rules and internal requirements rather than lend them, even at very high rates. The New York Fed carried out its first repo operations since 2008, then expanded them and began buying Treasury bills to rebuild reserves. The episode showed how thin the cushion of liquidity in the core dollar funding market had become, six months before the March 2020 dash for cash.

Details
Triggers and structural causes
  1. Trigger
    Cash out, collateral inOn 16 September, corporate tax payments moved roughly $35bn from bank and money-fund accounts into the Treasury General Account (TGA) at the Fed. On the same day, about $54bn of newly auctioned Treasuries settled. Dealers had to pay for them and then finance them in repo. Demand for cash rose just as its supply fell.
  2. Underlying
    Reserves no longer ampleTax dates and settlements happen regularly without incident. In 2019 they broke the market because the buffer of reserves had shrunk and was unevenly distributed.

In a simplified central-bank balance sheet, reserves are what remains after the Treasury's account and currency are subtracted from the Fed's assets:

$$ \text{Reserves} \;=\; \text{Fed securities \& loans} \;-\; \text{Currency} \;-\; \text{TGA} \;-\; \text{other liabilities}. $$

Quantitative tightening (shrinking the Fed's securities) and a rising TGA both drain reserves one for one, even if no bank chooses to hold less.

Structural causes in detail
  • Quantitative tightening. QE from 2008 to 2014 raised reserves to a peak of about $2.8tn. Balance-sheet runoff from October 2017 to August 2019 (ended early after the late-2018 market sell-off), together with a larger TGA, brought them down to about $1.4tn. The Fed believed this was still "ample". September showed it was close to the minimum the system needed.
  • Reserves were not truly "excess". Most reserves sat with a few of the largest banks. The liquidity coverage ratio treats reserves and Treasuries equally as high-quality liquid assets. In practice, banks preferred reserves because they can be used immediately for intraday payments, for resolution-planning liquidity requirements and to satisfy supervisors. Treasuries have to be sold or repoed first. The banks' internal minimum level of reserves was therefore much higher than the Fed had estimated.
  • Balance-sheet frictions. Money market funds, the main cash lenders, mostly lend through the Fed's reverse repo facility or to large dealers, not directly to hedge funds or smaller dealers. Large banks face balance-sheet costs (leverage ratio, G-SIB surcharges calculated largely on year-end values) that limit how much they are willing to intermediate, especially near reporting dates.
The role of hedge funds

Relative-value hedge funds had been growing their Treasury cash–futures basis trades, buying cash Treasuries financed overnight in repo and selling futures against them (see the basis-trade page). According to BIS analysis, their rising demand for repo financing was one of the pressures on rates in 2019. A sharp rise in repo rates squeezes the already thin margins of this trade. There is, however, little evidence of large-scale forced liquidation of Treasuries in September 2019; the major unwind came in March 2020. The Fed's intervention within a day kept financing costs from staying high long enough to force such sales.

Spillovers
  • SOFR. The Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the new benchmark replacing LIBOR, is computed from overnight Treasury repo trades. It printed 5.25% on 17 September, more than double its usual level. Few contracts referenced SOFR at the time, but the spike raised questions about its volatility as a benchmark and supported the later use of averaged and term SOFR rates.
  • Fed funds. The effective federal funds rate printed 2.30% on 17 September, above the 2.00–2.25% target range, a loss of control over the policy rate.
  • Other short-term markets. Higher repo rates briefly drew money funds away from commercial paper and CDs, and dealers' funding costs for bond inventories rose. The effects were modest and short-lived.
  • International. Foreign bank branches in the US, which often borrow in repo to hold reserves earning the IOER rate, had to adjust quickly. The cost of borrowing dollars through FX swaps rose somewhat, and concern about year-end dollar funding persisted until the Fed extended its operations.

Financial-stability reading
  • "Ample" reserves are hard to measure. Banks' demand for reserves depends on regulation, supervision and internal risk management, and it was much higher than the Fed had assumed. This episode shaped the Fed's later decision to operate with a larger balance sheet and to slow and stop QT earlier in later cycles.
  • Plumbing matters for monetary policy. A technical shortage of cash briefly cost the Fed control of its policy rate. Monetary policy implementation and financial stability are closely linked.
  • Regulation shapes market liquidity. Rules that make banks safer individually can make them less willing to redistribute cash when it is scarce. This is the same balance-sheet constraint that limited dealers in 2020.
  • Global dollar plumbing. Because the dollar repo market funds banks and investors worldwide, a local shortage spreads quickly through FX swaps and foreign bank branches. This had already shown how fragile the plumbing was before the much larger stress of 2020.

Oct – Dec 2018

Global equity and credit sell-off of late 2018#

Markets Policy / Regulation Geopolitics / Trade & Energy

In the last quarter of 2018, concerns about Federal Reserve tightening, the US–China trade conflict and slowing global growth combined into a broad repricing of risky assets. The S&P 500 fell almost 20% from its September peak to Christmas Eve, high-yield spreads widened by about 200bp, the US high-yield bond market shut for the whole of December, and oil fell by about 40%. The sell-off came in two phases. At first, rising US yields accompanied falling equities, as markets priced further rate hikes and continued balance-sheet reduction. Then yields fell as risk aversion and growth fears took over. No major financial institution failed, and market functioning held up. The episode is a useful example of a sharp tightening of financial conditions and correlated portfolio losses without a systemic banking crisis. It ended when the Fed signalled patience in January 2019, a shift that led to a pause in rate hikes, an early end to quantitative tightening, and rate cuts later that year.

Details
Background

By autumn 2018 the US economy was growing strongly, helped by the 2017 tax cuts. The Fed had raised rates eight times since December 2015 and was shrinking its balance sheet by up to $50bn a month. Outside the US, the picture was weaker: growth in China and Europe was slowing, emerging markets had come under pressure earlier in the year (notably Argentina and Turkey), and Italy's new government was in a budget dispute with the European Commission. On 24 September, the US imposed tariffs on a further $200bn of Chinese imports, and China retaliated.

Phase 1: rising yields and falling equities (October)

On 3 October, Fed Chair Jerome Powell said that rates were "a long way from neutral", suggesting more hikes than markets expected. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to about 3.2%, its highest since 2011. Higher discount rates hit richly valued growth and technology stocks first. October was the S&P 500's worst month since 2011, and global equities fell together. In this phase, bonds did not hedge equity losses, because both were reacting to the same rate shock.

Phase 2: risk-off and falling yields (November–December)

From November, the driver shifted from rates to growth. Oil prices fell sharply on oversupply and demand worries. Trade tensions persisted: the G20 truce of 1 December was quickly overshadowed by the arrest of a Huawei executive in Canada. On 3 December, part of the Treasury curve (2-year vs 5-year) inverted for the first time since 2007, reviving recession fears. Treasuries rallied as a safe haven, and the 10-year yield fell to about 2.7% by year-end, while equities and credit kept falling.

On 19 December the Fed raised rates for the fourth time in 2018, to 2.25–2.50%. Powell described balance-sheet runoff as being on "automatic pilot", which markets read as insensitive to tighter financial conditions. Selling intensified in thin holiday trading. Over the weekend of 22–23 December, a partial US government shutdown began, and Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin's publicised calls to bank CEOs to confirm their liquidity unsettled rather than calmed markets.

Credit markets
  • Spreads. US high-yield spreads widened from about 320bp in early October to about 530bp in late December. Investment-grade spreads also widened, with concern focused on the large share of BBB-rated debt close to junk status.
  • Primary market closed. No US high-yield bonds were issued in December 2018, the first month without issuance since November 2008.
  • Leveraged loans. Loan mutual funds and ETFs had record outflows and loan prices fell. This drew attention to the rapid growth of leveraged lending and collateralised loan obligations (CLOs), and to the liquidity mismatch of funds that offer daily redemptions on loans that take weeks to settle.

There was no crisis intervention: no emergency lending, asset purchases or market-functioning facilities were needed. The response came through monetary-policy guidance. Between July and October 2019 it cut rates three times. That shift also meant that QT stopped with reserves near the level at which the September 2019 repo spike occurred.

Financial-stability reading
  • Financial conditions can tighten without a crisis. Equity, credit and commodity prices fell sharply together, but banks were well capitalised, funding markets worked, and no institution failed. Losses fell mainly on end investors. The pattern closely resembled the 2015–16 China and commodity episode.
  • The stock–bond hedge depends on the shock. When the driver was higher rates, bonds and equities fell together. When it was growth fears, bonds hedged again. Diversification depends on whether a shock comes from rates or from growth, a pattern that returned strongly in 2022.
  • Feedback from markets to policy. The Fed's change of course was widely read as a response to tighter financial conditions. That reinforced the perception of a "Fed put" and raised questions about how far market moves should shape monetary policy.
  • Corporate credit and fund liquidity. The growth of BBB-rated debt, leveraged loans, and CLOs, and the liquidity mismatch of loan funds, became recurring themes in central-bank financial-stability reports in 2019, ahead of the stress of March 2020.
  • Thin markets amplify moves. Holiday-season liquidity, momentum and volatility-targeting strategies, and passive outflows helped make the December decline unusually abrupt. The same volatility-sensitive strategies had amplified the February 2018 volatility spike.

May 2018 – 2019

Italian sovereign-bond market stress#

Sovereign Markets Banking

After the March 2018 election, the formation of a government between the Five Star Movement and the League brought political and fiscal uncertainty back to the euro area's largest sovereign bond market. In late May, a leaked coalition draft that mentioned debt cancellation and euro exit, followed by a constitutional clash over the choice of finance minister, triggered a sharp repricing. On 29 May the 2-year BTP yield recorded its largest one-day rise in decades, and market depth largely disappeared. Unlike a short-lived flash event, the stress returned in the autumn. The government's 2.4% deficit target led to the European Commission's first-ever rejection of a draft budget, a rating downgrade, and a 10-year spread over German Bunds above 300bp. Liquidity deteriorated again each time. Italian banks, large holders of domestic government debt, saw their share prices and funding costs move with the sovereign. The episode showed that sovereign-market liquidity and the link between banks and their sovereign remained vulnerable inside the monetary union, even after the euro-crisis reforms.

Details
Background

Italy entered 2018 with public debt of about 130% of GDP, weak growth and a banking sector still reducing a large stock of non-performing loans. The ECB's asset purchase programme had kept yields low, but net purchases were being scaled down and were due to end in December 2018. The 4 March election produced no majority. In May the anti-establishment Five Star Movement and the League began negotiating a coalition programme that included large tax cuts, a basic income and a reversal of pension reform.

Bank–sovereign links
  • Large domestic holdings. Italian banks held around €370–390bn of Italian government bonds, roughly a tenth of their assets. (to verify) Falling bond prices reduced their capital, directly through securities held at fair value and indirectly through higher funding costs.
  • Bank equity and funding. Italian bank shares fell sharply during both phases, and banks' wholesale funding costs rose in line with the sovereign spread. Banks issued little unsecured debt in the second half of 2018.
  • Feedback loop. Banks depend on the sovereign's credibility, and the sovereign depends on banks as buyers of its debt. This is the "doom loop" of the 2010–12 euro crisis. Banking union had not removed it, because there was no common deposit insurance and no limit on banks' holdings of domestic sovereign debt.
Policy context

There was no crisis intervention. The ECB ended net asset purchases as planned in December 2018 but continued to reinvest maturing bonds. It made clear that its Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) instrument would require a country to accept a European support programme, which the Italian government ruled out. In March 2019 the ECB announced a new series of targeted long-term loans to banks (TLTRO-III). This was justified by monetary policy, but it also eased funding pressure on banks in more fragile countries, including Italy.

Financial-stability reading
  • Sovereign-bond liquidity is fragile. Even one of the world's largest government bond markets can lose depth within hours when dealers step back. Unlike a flash event, the impairment returned with each new round of political news. The 2015 Bund tantrum had shown the same fragility in the euro area's safest bond market.
  • Redenomination risk did not disappear with OMT. Markets still priced a probability of euro exit in a large member state when its government questioned euro membership.
  • The bank–sovereign nexus persists. Concentrated domestic sovereign holdings meant that political shocks quickly reached bank capital and funding. This kept alive the debate on a European deposit insurance scheme and on limiting sovereign concentration in bank portfolios.
  • Contained by fundamentals and institutions. Italy had a primary surplus, long average debt maturity and large domestic ownership, and the ECB remained present in the market through reinvestments. Together these limited contagion to other euro-area sovereigns.
  • A preview of later stress. BTP spreads widened sharply again in March 2020, until the ECB's pandemic purchase programme was announced.

"Volmageddon": the short-volatility unwind#

Markets Non-bank

After more than a year of unusually calm markets, a modest equity correction in early February 2018 produced an exceptionally large jump in volatility. On 5 February the S&P 500 fell about 4.1% and the VIX rose by about 20 points, from 17.3 to 37.3. This was the largest one-day rise in the index's history in both points and percentage terms. The move was amplified by exchange-traded products (ETPs) that bet on low volatility, or offered leveraged exposure to it, through VIX futures. These products rebalance mechanically at the end of each day. When volatility rose, both inverse and leveraged products had to buy VIX futures in the last minutes of trading, pushing volatility higher still. The largest inverse product, Credit Suisse's XIV, lost about 96% of its value overnight and was terminated. Volatility-targeting and risk-parity funds then cut equity exposure as measured volatility rose, extending the sell-off. By 8 February the S&P 500 was more than 10% below its January peak. No institution failed, and the losses fell on end investors. The episode is a clear early example of a pattern that recurs in later non-bank episodes: investment strategies can amplify the very market variable on which their exposures depend.

Details
Background: the low-volatility regime

In 2017 the VIX averaged about 11, its lowest annual average on record, and closed below 10 on dozens of days. (to verify) After the 2015–16 China and commodity sell-off faded, steady global growth, low rates and central-bank asset purchases had kept realised volatility very low. Selling volatility had become a popular and profitable strategy. Short-volatility ETPs gained steadily because VIX futures usually trade above the spot VIX and drift down towards it as they approach expiry. Retail investors, hedge funds and some asset managers had built up exposure. A larger body of strategies was implicitly short volatility, because their equity exposure depended on volatility staying low: volatility-targeting funds, risk-parity portfolios, and some option-selling and trend-following (CTA) strategies.

Trigger

On 2 February, US payroll data showed average hourly earnings up 2.9% on the year, the fastest pace since 2009. Investors began to fear that inflation and Fed tightening would arrive faster than expected. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to about 2.85%, a four-year high, and the S&P 500 fell about 2.1%. On the following Monday, 5 February, selling accelerated in the afternoon. The Dow fell 1,175 points, then its largest one-day point decline on record, and about 4.6% in percentage terms.

Mechanism: why volatility products buy volatility

A daily-rebalanced ETP with leverage factor \(\beta\) promises \(\beta\) times the daily return \(r\) on a VIX futures index. Here \(\beta = -1\) for an inverse product and \(\beta = 2\) for a leveraged one. To keep its exposure equal to \(\beta \times \mathrm{NAV}\), it must trade futures at the close by roughly

$$ \Delta \text{Exposure} \;\approx\; \beta\,(\beta - 1)\cdot \mathrm{NAV}\cdot r . $$

For both \(\beta = -1\) and \(\beta = 2\), the factor \(\beta(\beta-1)\) equals \(+2\). Inverse and leveraged products therefore both need to buy VIX futures when futures prices rise, and to buy more the larger the rise. Their rebalancing is pro-cyclical with respect to volatility. The trades were also predictable. They were concentrated in the short window around the 4:00–4:15pm close, in a futures market whose depth was small relative to the products' needs.

  1. Equities fall and the VIX and VIX futures rise.
  2. Inverse and leveraged ETPs lose value and must buy VIX futures at the close to rebalance.
  3. Concentrated buying in a thin market drives VIX futures sharply higher.
  4. Higher measured volatility forces volatility-targeting and risk-parity funds to cut equity exposure, and dealers hedging short option positions sell futures. Equities fall further.

On 5 February the short-term VIX futures index rose by roughly 96%, much of it in the last hour of trading and after the equity close. Because the inverse products' rebalancing need was known in advance, other traders could position ahead of it, which deepened the move. Financial-stability reading
  • Strategies that move their own risk factor. The products' exposure was to volatility, and their rebalancing raised volatility. The same structure, with forced flows feeding back into the price that triggers them, recurs in the 2022 LDI spiral and in margin-driven deleveraging in March 2020.
  • Predictable, crowded flows. Mechanical rules that are public and concentrated in time invite front-running and turn a thin market into a trap. Market depth matters relative to the size of forced trades, not in absolute terms. Retail leverage had already been wiped out in a similar way by the Swiss-franc shock of 2015.
  • Low volatility breeds fragility. Long calm periods encourage leverage scaled to recent volatility. Measured risk falls, positions grow, and the eventual shock is larger. This is the "volatility paradox", which had appeared in bond markets in the 2015 Bund tantrum.
  • Hidden short-volatility exposure. The explicit short-volatility ETPs were small, a few billion dollars. Much larger pools of capital held the same exposure implicitly, through volatility targeting and risk parity, and this was hard for supervisors to measure.
  • Losses without a systemic crisis. The products failed as designed. Their prospectuses allowed termination, and investors bore the losses. Because no bank leverage or runnable funding was involved, the shock did not spread to the financial system. Later in the year, the late-2018 sell-off showed how these strategies interact with a broader tightening of financial conditions.

The Brexit referendum shock#

Markets Non-bank Policy / Regulation Geopolitics / Trade & Energy

On 23 June 2016 the United Kingdom voted by 51.9% to 48.1% to leave the European Union. Polls and betting markets had pointed to a Remain win, so the result forced a sudden reassessment of UK and global prospects. As results came in overnight, sterling fell from about $1.50 to $1.32, its lowest level since 1985 and its largest one-day fall in the era of floating exchange rates. Over the next two trading days, major advanced-economy equity indices fell by more than 5%. The domestically focused FTSE 250 fell almost 15%, and shares in UK banks and housebuilders fell by up to a third. Investors moved into gilts, Bunds, US Treasuries, the yen and gold, and the 10-year gilt yield fell below 1% for the first time. Within two weeks, several UK open-ended commercial property funds suspended redemptions. Banks entered the shock well capitalised, and the Bank of England had prepared publicly, so funding markets kept working. This was a major political shock and a test of the post-2008 reforms, not a repeat of the 2008 funding collapse. The Bank later released the countercyclical capital buffer and in August eased monetary policy with a broad package.

Details
Background: a known date, an unexpected outcome

Unlike most crises in this chronology, the date of the shock was known months in advance. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee had identified the referendum as the most significant near-term domestic risk to financial stability. Sterling options showed record implied volatility over the vote date, and many banks and investors had put contingency plans in place. What markets had not priced was the outcome. On the eve of the vote, betting odds implied a probability of Remain of roughly 80–90% (to verify), and sterling rallied as the polls closed. The losses came from that gap between expectations and the result.

Market transmission
  • Sterling. The pound became the main shock absorber. It fell by about 8% intraday against the dollar on 24 June and kept falling over the following months. In the early hours of 7 October, a flash crash in thin Asian trading took it about 6% lower in minutes, briefly to around $1.18 on major platforms. A BIS review attributed the move to illiquid trading hours, stop-loss and options-related selling, and algorithmic trading. (verify levels)
  • UK equities. The FTSE 100 fell almost 9% in early trading on 24 June but closed about 3% lower. It recovered above its pre-referendum level within days, because a weaker pound raised the sterling value of its constituents' foreign earnings. The FTSE 250, which is more exposed to the domestic economy, fell about 7% on 24 June and almost 15% over two days.
  • Banks and property. Barclays and Lloyds lost around a third of their value in early trading on 24 June. On 27 June, trading in Barclays and RBS shares was repeatedly halted by circuit breakers. Housebuilders and listed property companies also fell sharply. (intraday figures to verify)
  • Global equities. Euro-area markets fell more than the UK on 24 June. The Euro Stoxx 50 lost about 8.6%, and Italy's and Spain's main indices lost about 12%, as Italian and Spanish banks fell sharply. The Nikkei 225 fell almost 8% and the S&P 500 about 3.6%. Global equities lost roughly $2tn of market value on 24 June, the largest one-day loss on record at the time. (to verify)
  • Safe havens. Gilt, Bund and Treasury yields fell, and the 10-year Bund yield turned negative. The yen briefly traded stronger than ¥100 per dollar. Gold rose by about 5%, and the Swiss National Bank intervened to limit the franc's rise.

Non-bank stress: open-ended property funds

The clearest liquidity mismatch was in UK open-ended commercial property funds. They offered daily dealing to retail investors while holding office buildings, shopping centres and warehouses that take months to sell. After the vote, investors rushed to redeem, fearing a fall in property values. Selling buildings quickly enough would have meant fire sales, so funds suspended dealing. Within days, funds holding around £18bn of assets had gated (to verify). Other funds stayed open but cut the price paid to exiting investors by 5–15% to discourage outflows.

The suspensions protected investors who stayed, and they were lifted over the following months. They also revealed a structural first-mover advantage. The episode fed into the FSB's 2017 recommendations on open-ended fund liquidity and into new UK rules on funds investing in inherently illiquid assets (2019). Property funds were gated again in March 2020, when valuers could not price their assets. (verify dates)

Policy response
  • Pre-announced liquidity. The Bank had prepared its operations in advance and stated them within hours of the result. The aim was to reassure markets that liquidity would not run out, as it had in 2007–08. Take-up of the additional sterling and dollar operations was modest. Markets needed the assurance more than the funds. (to verify)
  • Macroprudential release. In March 2016 the FPC had decided to raise the countercyclical buffer from 0% to 0.5%, effective 2017. On 5 July it reversed this and set the buffer at 0%. The FPC estimated that this freed about £5.7bn of capital, enough to support up to £150bn of lending. It was an early use of a post-2008 macroprudential buffer to absorb a shock rather than to build resilience.
  • Monetary easing. The August package combined a rate cut, renewed gilt purchases and the Bank's first corporate bond purchases. It also introduced the Term Funding Scheme, which gave banks cheap four-year funding linked to lending, so that the lower Bank Rate would pass through to borrowers.
  • Elsewhere. The ECB and the Fed said they were ready to provide liquidity through swap lines. The SNB intervened in FX markets. The Fed postponed further rate rises, citing global risks, and its next hike came in December 2016.
Financial-stability reading
  • The post-2008 reforms passed a live test. UK banks held much more capital and liquidity than in 2007. They had been stress-tested against harsher scenarios and continued to lend. Equity prices fell sharply, but funding markets did not seize up and no institution needed support.
  • Preparation and communication work. The Bank announced its contingency plans in advance and confirmed them immediately. That helped prevent a precautionary hoarding of liquidity. The template is useful for any shock that can be scheduled, such as a vote or a debt ceiling deadline.
  • The currency absorbed the shock. A floating exchange rate absorbed much of the adjustment, and gilts rallied. In the 2022 gilt crisis, sterling and gilts fell together. That combination signalled a loss of confidence in UK policy itself rather than a shock to growth.
  • Liquidity mismatch outside banks. The property-fund gates were the main financial-stability event. They show how promising daily liquidity on illiquid assets turns a shift in sentiment into a run. A US high-yield fund, Third Avenue, had gated for the same reason during the 2015–16 sell-off.
  • Thin markets and flash events. The October sterling flash crash showed that even a major currency can gap sharply when liquidity is thin and automated selling is concentrated. The franc had gapped far more after the removal of the SNB floor.
  • Slow-moving rather than acute risks. The lasting effects ran through lower investment, a weaker currency and higher inflation, and through years of uncertainty about cross-border financial services. These ran on a much longer timescale than the market shock.

Jun 2015 – Feb 2016

China's equity bust, the renminbi devaluation and the 2015–16 global risk-off#

Markets Non-bank Policy / Regulation Geopolitics / Trade & Energy

Between mid-2014 and June 2015, Chinese equities more than doubled, driven by retail investors and by leverage, much of it from lightly regulated "shadow" margin lending. When regulators moved against informal margin finance, the boom unwound. The Shanghai Composite fell about 30% in three weeks, and the state responded with an extraordinary rescue: share purchases, a ban on large shareholders selling, and trading suspensions covering about half of all listed firms. On 11 August the People's Bank of China changed how it set the renminbi's daily fixing, and the currency fell 1.9% in a day, its largest move in two decades. Markets read the change as a sign of weak growth and a possible move towards devaluation. On 24 August, "Black Monday", Shanghai fell 8.5%, US equity markets opened in disorder, and the VIX briefly exceeded 50. Capital outflows drained China's foreign-exchange reserves by about $500bn in 2015. A second wave came in early 2016. A new Chinese circuit breaker closed the market twice in four days, oil fell below $30, energy credit and commodity exporters came under strain, and European bank shares and bonds sold off. Markets stabilised from mid-February, as China steadied the renminbi and the Fed, the ECB and other central banks eased or slowed their tightening. The episode linked domestic leverage, exchange-rate uncertainty, commodity-exporter vulnerabilities and global portfolio adjustment, and it showed that shocks from China had become global shocks.

Details
Background

By 2015 China's growth was slowing from the double-digit rates of the previous decade. Its total non-financial debt had risen from about 140% of GDP in 2008 to more than 230%, much of it held by state-linked companies and channelled through the shadow banking system. (to verify) Commodity prices were already falling. Brent had dropped from about $115 in June 2014 to below $60, as US shale supply grew and OPEC chose not to cut output. China's slowdown reduced demand for metals and energy. Meanwhile, the Federal Reserve was preparing its first rate rise since 2006, and the dollar had risen strongly. Many emerging-market firms had borrowed heavily in dollars during the years of near-zero US rates.

1 · The Chinese equity boom and bust (June–August 2015)#
A leveraged retail boom

From mid-2014, rate cuts, official media commentary that presented rising shares as a sign of reform, and the opening of the Shanghai–Hong Kong Stock Connect drew millions of new retail investors into the market. The Shanghai Composite rose about 150% in a year and peaked at 5,166 on 12 June 2015. Leverage came through two channels:

  • Formal margin lending by securities firms, which reached about RMB 2.3tn in mid-June.
  • Informal ("grey-market") margin finance, provided through umbrella trusts, peer-to-peer platforms and trading software that let investors borrow at leverage of up to 5–10×. Estimates of its size ranged from RMB 0.5tn to 1.5tn. (to verify)

In mid-June the China Securities Regulatory Commission tightened rules on informal margin finance. Highly leveraged positions began to hit their liquidation levels, and the market fell about 30% in three weeks.

2 · The renminbi fixing change and "Black Monday" (August 2015)#
The 11 August reform

The renminbi could trade within 2% either side of a daily "central parity" set by the PBoC. Before August 2015, the fixing was largely discretionary and had kept the currency stable against a rising dollar. On 11 August the PBoC announced that the fixing would take more account of the previous day's closing rate, a market-oriented reform that the IMF had encouraged ahead of its decision on adding the renminbi to the SDR basket. The currency fell 1.9% that day, and about 3% over three days.

The move was small, but its timing and communication made it a global shock. With growth slowing and equities already falling, markets read it as a sign that the authorities were worried about growth, and as a possible first step towards a much larger devaluation. Emerging-market currencies fell, especially those of Asian trading partners and commodity exporters. Kazakhstan floated its currency on 20 August, and the tenge fell by about a quarter in a day.

Capital outflows and reserve losses

Expectations of depreciation encouraged Chinese households and firms to move money abroad and to repay dollar debt early. To defend the currency, the PBoC sold reserves:

  1. Markets expect the renminbi to weaken.
  2. Firms repay dollar debt and residents move savings abroad.
  3. The PBoC sells reserves to slow the depreciation, and domestic liquidity tightens.
  4. Falling reserves are read as a sign of pressure, reinforcing depreciation expectations.

Reserves fell by about $510bn in 2015, including a record fall of about $108bn in December. By January 2016 they were about $800bn below their mid-2014 peak of almost $4tn. The authorities tightened capital controls on outflows, and on 11 December the PBoC began publishing the renminbi's value against a basket of currencies (the CFETS index), to shift attention away from the dollar rate. (reserve figures to verify)

Black Monday: 24 August 2015
  • China. The Shanghai Composite fell 8.5%, its largest one-day fall since 2007, and wiped out its gains for the year. State media called it "Black Monday". The PBoC cut interest rates and reserve requirements the following day.
  • US market structure under stress. The Dow fell about 1,089 points in the first minutes of trading. Many stocks opened late, and single-stock "limit up–limit down" trading pauses were triggered more than 1,200 times (to verify). Some large exchange-traded funds traded at deep discounts to the value of their holdings, and stop-loss orders were executed at very low prices. The VIX rose above 50 intraday and closed at about 40.7.
  • Fed delay. In September the Fed chose not to raise rates, citing global economic and financial developments. It made its first hike in December 2015.
3 · The early-2016 sell-off (January–February 2016)#
China's circuit breaker

On 4 January 2016 China introduced a market-wide circuit breaker. A 5% fall in the CSI 300 index would halt trading for 15 minutes, and a 7% fall would close the market for the day. It was triggered on its first day, and on 7 January the market closed after about half an hour of trading. As the index neared the 5% threshold, investors sold to get out before trading stopped, so the rule hastened the falls it was meant to prevent. It was suspended on 8 January. Weaker renminbi fixings in the same week renewed fears of a devaluation.

Commodities, credit and exporters
  • Oil. Brent fell below $28 on 20 January and WTI reached about $26 on 11 February, lows not seen since 2003 and down about 75% from mid-2014. Industrial metals also fell.
  • Energy credit. US high-yield energy bonds came under heavy selling and defaults by shale producers rose. The overall US high-yield spread reached almost 900bp on 11 February. (to verify) In December 2015, Third Avenue's Focused Credit Fund, a mutual fund holding illiquid, low-rated bonds, had blocked redemptions and moved to liquidate, rather than sell assets at fire-sale prices.
  • Commodity exporters. Currencies of exporters such as Brazil, Russia, South Africa, Colombia and Canada fell sharply. Brazil lost its investment-grade rating (S&P, September 2015; Fitch, December 2015). Oil exporters drew on sovereign wealth funds, and Saudi Arabia and others sold foreign assets to finance budget deficits.
European banks

In early February the pressure turned to European banks. The fall in growth expectations and the Bank of Japan's move to negative rates on 29 January led markets to doubt that banks could earn enough to cover their costs. Bank shares fell about a quarter between the start of the year and mid-February. (to verify) Concern focused on Deutsche Bank's ability to pay coupons on its contingent-convertible (AT1) bonds, whose prices fell sharply. The bank stated publicly that it had ample capacity to pay. The episode was the first market test of AT1 coupon risk, and a precursor to the much larger Credit Suisse AT1 write-down of 2023.

Policy response
  • China. Direct state support of the equity market, rate and reserve-requirement cuts, heavy foreign-exchange intervention, tighter capital controls and fiscal and credit stimulus. The authorities reversed course on the circuit breaker after four days.
  • Advanced economies. No emergency lending was needed. The response came through monetary policy: the Fed delayed and then slowed its tightening, and the ECB and Bank of Japan eased further.
  • Market structure. After 24 August, US regulators and exchanges reviewed the opening process and the rules for single-stock trading pauses. (To be completed: SEC staff research note, Dec 2015, and later changes to LULD rules.)
Financial-stability reading
  • China as a source of global shocks. China's domestic financial system was largely closed to foreign investors. Its stress still spread worldwide, through trade, commodity prices, exchange-rate expectations and investors' view of global growth, rather than through direct financial exposures.
  • Shadow leverage in retail markets. Leverage outside the regulated broker system drove the boom. When it was cut off, mechanical liquidations drove the bust. This is the same fire-sale mechanism seen in hedge funds and LDI funds, in a market dominated by households.
  • Communication matters for exchange-rate policy. A small, technically justified change to the fixing caused a large shock, because markets were unsure what the authorities wanted.
  • Reserves are a buffer, but runs drain them. China's large reserves allowed it to defend the currency, but at a cost of hundreds of billions of dollars, and only with tighter capital controls.
  • Market-structure safeguards can backfire. China's circuit breaker and the trading pauses of 24 August both showed that mechanisms intended to calm markets can add to stress when they create a rush for the exit or prevent prices from forming.
  • Funds that offer daily liquidity on illiquid credit. The Third Avenue gate was small, but it raised the same liquidity-mismatch concern as the UK property funds after the Brexit vote a few months later.
  • Commodity exporters and dollar debt. Falling commodity prices, a rising dollar and the prospect of Fed tightening hit exporters and dollar borrowers together. This pattern returned in the late-2018 sell-off.

The Bund tantrum#

Sovereign Markets

In March 2015 the ECB began buying euro-area government bonds at a pace of about €60bn a month. German yields, already very low, fell further, and on 17 April the 10-year Bund yield reached about 0.05%. Much of the shorter end of the curve traded at negative yields. Within weeks the move reversed with unusual force. By 7 May the 10-year yield was close to 0.8%, and by 10 June it was near 1%, a rise of roughly 95bp in under two months. Intraday swings of 15–20bp became common in a market where daily moves of a few basis points had been the norm. Dealers stepped back, market depth thinned and bid-ask spreads widened, and the sell-off spread to US Treasuries, gilts and other sovereign markets. No institution failed, and there was no credit concern about Germany. The episode showed that low credit risk does not guarantee stable market liquidity. A crowded, one-way trade in the world's benchmark safe asset, combined with scarce bonds and thin dealer capacity, could still produce a sharp dislocation in a core sovereign market.

Details
Background: QE and the scarcity of Bunds

The euro area faced very low inflation in late 2014, and briefly deflation in early 2015, as oil prices collapsed. On 22 January the ECB announced its expanded asset purchase programme, a move anticipated a week earlier by the Swiss National Bank's abandonment of its franc floor. Purchases of government bonds under the PSPP began on 9 March. Purchases were allocated according to each country's share of the ECB's capital, so Germany, with the largest share, saw the largest purchases. Germany was also running a budget surplus, so net issuance of Bunds was small.

The ECB's deposit rate was −0.20%. Bonds yielding less than that rate were initially ineligible for purchase, which concentrated buying in the longer maturities that still qualified. Investors, including banks and insurers that need high-quality collateral, had to compete with the central bank for a shrinking supply of free-floating Bunds. Many bought ahead of the ECB. By mid-April, yields on Bunds up to about nine years' maturity were negative, and positioning was heavily one-sided. (share of Bunds below the deposit rate to verify)

Trigger: no single shock

There was no clear trigger, which is part of what made the episode notable. Several developments in late April and May led investors to question whether yields near zero were sustainable:

  • Inflation expectations. Oil prices rebounded from their January lows, and euro-area inflation data surprised on the upside. Market measures of inflation expectations rose.
  • Positioning and sentiment. On 21 April, bond investor Bill Gross described short-selling Bunds as "the short of a lifetime". This was widely cited as a sign of how stretched valuations had become.
  • Supply and data. Weak demand at some German auctions, stronger euro-area growth data and the Greek negotiations of 2015 added to the uncertainty.
Mechanism: why the reversal was so violent

At very low yields, long-dated bonds are highly sensitive to rate changes. For a bond with modified duration \(D\), a yield rise \(\Delta y\) causes a price change of roughly

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \;\approx\; -\,D \cdot \Delta y . $$

A 10-year Bund with a coupon near zero has a duration of almost 10. A rise of about 95bp therefore meant a price fall of roughly 9%, a very large loss for an asset held for safety. At a yield of 0.05%, the coupon gave almost no cushion against such losses. Several channels amplified the move:

  1. Yields rise from very low levels, and losses on crowded long positions mount.
  2. Measured volatility jumps. Value-at-risk (VaR) and other volatility-based risk limits tighten, so investors must cut duration even without any change in their views.
  3. Dealers, with limited balance-sheet capacity, widen bid-ask spreads and cut the size they quote rather than absorb the selling.
  4. In thin markets, the forced selling moves prices further, and volatility rises again.

This is the "VaR shock" dynamic that had appeared in Japanese government bonds in 2003. Volatility-based limits turn a rise in volatility into forced selling, the same structure seen in the 2018 short-volatility unwind. Bund futures, the most liquid instrument, carried much of the adjustment, and large orders moved prices sharply. Scarcity of the underlying bonds also made them expensive to borrow in repo, which made it harder for investors to take the other side of the trade.

Spillovers
  • Other sovereign markets. The sell-off spread to US Treasuries, UK gilts, Japanese government bonds and other euro-area sovereigns. Periphery spreads over Bunds widened only modestly. The driver was a repricing of the core safe asset, not a credit scare. The value of global bond holdings fell by several hundred billion dollars. (estimate to source)
  • Investors holding duration. Insurers, pension funds and bond funds saw large mark-to-market losses on assets they regarded as among the safest available.
  • Other currencies and assets. The euro strengthened as yields rose, and equity markets in the euro area fell back from their spring highs.
Policy response

There was no intervention. The ECB kept its purchases unchanged and stated that it would not respond to higher volatility. In December 2015 it extended the programme and allowed purchases of bonds yielding below the deposit rate, which eased the scarcity of eligible Bunds. (verify date of eligibility change; the yield floor was removed in Dec 2016, effective Jan 2017) Central banks and international bodies took up the episode in their analysis of market liquidity, alongside the US Treasury "flash rally" of 15 October 2014, when the 10-year Treasury yield moved about 37bp intraday without obvious news.

Financial-stability reading
  • Low credit risk does not mean stable liquidity. Germany's creditworthiness was never in doubt. The dislocation came from positioning, scarcity and dealers' limited capacity to absorb one-way selling.
  • Low yields raise price risk. At yields near zero, duration is high and the coupon offers no buffer. Small changes in yield expectations become large capital losses, and "safe" assets can show equity-like price moves.
  • Liquidity can be abundant until it is needed. Market depth that is ample in normal conditions can disappear quickly. The episode was part of the evidence behind concerns about post-2008 dealer capacity, which became central in March 2020.
  • Central-bank purchases shape market structure. QE reduced the supply of bonds available to trade and lend. That can thin markets and make price moves sharper when sentiment turns.
  • Volatility-based risk management is pro-cyclical. When limits depend on recent volatility, a jump in volatility forces selling. Calm periods encourage larger positions, and those positions then have to be cut together.
  • A precursor to later sovereign stress. The same features returned in other core markets: liquidity that evaporated in Italian BTPs in 2018, and forced selling of long-dated bonds in the 2022 gilt crisis.

The Swiss National Bank abandons the franc floor#

Markets Non-bank Policy / Regulation

Since September 2011 the Swiss National Bank had capped the franc's value with a minimum exchange rate of CHF 1.20 per euro, defended by unlimited foreign-currency purchases. Its officials had described the floor as a cornerstone of policy as recently as that week. At about 10:30 CET on 15 January 2015, without warning, the SNB announced that the floor was discontinued and cut its deposit rate to −0.75%. Within minutes the franc rose by roughly 30% against the euro. EUR/CHF fell from 1.20 to below 0.90 before ending the day near 1.04, one of the largest moves in a major currency since the end of Bretton Woods. For several minutes there were almost no prices at which to trade. Stop-loss orders were executed far from their trigger levels, and many leveraged positions lost more than the collateral posted against them. The losses passed from retail traders to their brokers and from brokers to their prime-brokerage banks. FXCM, the largest US retail FX broker, needed a $300m emergency loan within a day, and Alpari UK and Global Brokers NZ became insolvent. Several banks and hedge funds reported losses, and borrowers with franc-denominated mortgages in Poland and elsewhere in central and eastern Europe saw their debts jump. The episode is a clear example of a break in policy regime interacting with crowded positioning and leverage when prices move discontinuously.

Details
Background: a one-sided peg

During the euro-area crisis, investors bought francs as a safe haven, and by August 2011 the franc was close to parity with the euro. The resulting deflationary pressure on Swiss exporters led the SNB, on 6 September 2011, to set a floor of CHF 1.20 per euro. It said it would buy "foreign currency in unlimited quantities" to enforce it. Because the SNB can create francs without limit, the promise was credible in one direction. Its cost appeared on the balance sheet: foreign-exchange reserves rose to about CHF 500bn, roughly 75–80% of Swiss GDP. (to verify)

The floor made EUR/CHF one of the least volatile exchange rates in the world. For three years it traded mostly between 1.20 and 1.25. Market participants treated 1.20 as a near-certain lower bound and wrote few options against a break below it. Retail traders and some funds held long EUR/CHF positions on high leverage, effectively selling insurance against the floor's removal for a small, steady return. Households and firms in Poland, Hungary, Croatia and Romania had borrowed in francs since the mid-2000s to obtain lower interest rates, and the floor limited their currency risk.

Why the floor became untenable
  • ECB quantitative easing. By January 2015 markets expected the ECB to announce large-scale purchases of government bonds, which it did on 22 January. That would weaken the euro and could require much larger SNB interventions to keep EUR/CHF at 1.20.
  • A weakening euro. The euro was falling against the dollar. Holding the franc at a fixed rate to the euro meant the franc was also weakening against the dollar, which reduced the case for the floor.
  • Balance-sheet risk. Each intervention added to the SNB's foreign-currency holdings and to its exposure to future franc appreciation. This was politically sensitive, since the SNB distributes profits to the Confederation and the cantons.

A floor that is expected to be abandoned invites a speculative attack, so the SNB had a strong incentive to give no warning. Its public statements of commitment were part of the defence, until the day they were dropped.

Mechanism: leverage meets a price gap

Margin and stop-loss systems assume that prices move continuously, so that positions can be closed out before losses exceed collateral. On 15 January the price jumped. Liquidity providers withdrew their quotes, electronic platforms showed few or no prices for several minutes, and orders were filled once trading resumed at levels far below the stop. For a position with leverage \(L\) (notional ÷ margin) and a price move \(\Delta S / S\), the loss as a multiple of the margin posted is

$$ \frac{\text{Loss}}{\text{Margin}} \;\approx\; L \cdot \frac{\Delta S}{S} . $$

Retail FX accounts commonly offered leverage of 50:1 or more. With \(L = 50\) and a move of about 20% at the point where orders were executed, the loss is about 10 times the margin. The client's account becomes negative, and the broker is left with a claim that it is unlikely to recover. The loss chain ran in sequence:

  1. Client
    Account wiped outLeveraged long EUR/CHF positions lose all their margin and go into negative balance.
  2. Broker
    Absorbs the shortfallRetail brokers have hedged client positions with their own liquidity providers, so they must pay the full loss to the banks even when clients cannot pay them.
  3. Prime broker
    Counterparty lossWhen a broker or fund cannot meet its obligations, the bank that gave it credit takes the loss.

Losses and spillovers
  • Retail FX brokers. FXCM's share price fell about 90% in a day, and the terms of the Leucadia loan left its lender with most of the firm's future value. Interactive Brokers reported a loss of about $120m, mostly from customers who could not cover their deficits. (figures to verify)
  • Banks. Citigroup, Deutsche Bank and Barclays were reported to have lost about $100–150m each, mainly through trading and client defaults. (to verify) The losses were small relative to their capital.
  • Hedge funds. Some macro funds with large long-euro positions suffered heavy losses. Everest Capital's main fund, which had about $800m of assets, was reported to have lost almost all of them and closed. (to verify)
  • Franc-denominated mortgages. In Poland, households held franc mortgages worth around PLN 130–140bn. (to verify) Their value in zloty rose about 15–20% overnight. Courts and governments in several countries later forced or encouraged conversions, and in Poland litigation over these loans became a large and lasting cost for banks. Hungary had already converted most franc mortgages into forints in late 2014.
  • The Swiss economy and the SNB. Exporters and tourism faced a sudden loss of competitiveness. The SNB recorded a large loss on its reserves in 2015 and continued to intervene, in smaller amounts and at its discretion, in later years.
Policy response

The central-bank decision was itself the shock, and there was no rescue or support from public authorities. The lasting response came from conduct regulators. FX brokers and banks raised margin requirements on currencies with managed exchange rates. Regulators turned their attention to retail leveraged products. In 2018 ESMA imposed EU-wide restrictions on contracts for difference sold to retail clients, including leverage caps of 30:1 on major currency pairs and mandatory negative-balance protection, so that retail clients cannot lose more than their deposit. The UK FCA adopted equivalent permanent rules in 2019. (verify dates and caps)

Financial-stability reading
  • Policy regimes can end abruptly. A commitment that markets treat as permanent suppresses volatility and encourages leverage. Its removal is a discontinuous event that risk models calibrated on recent history do not capture.
  • Stop-losses and margin assume continuous prices. When prices gap, collateral can fall short of losses, and credit risk appears in arrangements designed to be fully collateralised.
  • Losses move up the chain. Retail losses became broker losses, and broker losses became bank losses. The chain was short and the banks were well capitalised, but the mechanism is the same counterparty channel later seen with Archegos.
  • Crowded positioning in "safe" trades. A trade with small, regular gains and a rare, very large loss attracts leverage. This is the pattern of the 2018 short-volatility unwind.
  • Liquidity disappears when it is needed. Even in one of the most traded markets, liquidity providers withdrew within seconds, anticipating the shorter-lived sterling flash crash of 2016.
  • Household currency mismatch. Foreign-currency borrowing by households converts exchange-rate shocks into credit losses and, eventually, into legal and political risk for banks.

15 Oct 2014

The US Treasury "flash rally"#

Markets Sovereign

On the morning of 15 October 2014, the yield on the 10-year US Treasury note fell by about 16bp in roughly twelve minutes, between 9:33 and 9:45 ET, and then reversed almost entirely by 10:00. Over the day it traded in a range of about 37bp, one of the widest since 2009, although it closed only a few basis points lower. Weak retail sales data had been released at 8:30, but the size and speed of the move far exceeded any reasonable reaction to the news. During the event window, trading volume was very high while the visible depth of the order book collapsed. Bank dealers widened their quotes or withdrew, and principal trading firms using automated strategies accounted for most of the trading. The joint review by the US Treasury, the Federal Reserve, the SEC and the CFTC, published in July 2015, found no single cause. It did find significantly strained liquidity conditions and a market structure that had changed faster than official monitoring of it. The episode was an early warning about the resilience of the world's benchmark sovereign market, and a precursor to the much larger stress of March 2020.

Details
Background

In October 2014 the Fed was about to complete the asset purchases of its third round of QE, which ended on 29 October, and markets expected rate rises in 2015. Many investors held short duration positions, betting that yields would rise. Global growth concerns, falling oil prices, weak European data and fears about Ebola had already pushed yields lower and volatility higher in the first half of the month. (positioning data to source, e.g. CFTC Commitments of Traders)

The structure of the market had also changed. Trading in on-the-run Treasuries and Treasury futures had moved largely onto electronic platforms, where principal trading firms using high-speed automated strategies provided much of the liquidity. Bank dealers, constrained by post-2008 capital and leverage rules, held smaller inventories relative to the size of the market.

Trigger and the event window
  1. 8:30 ET
    Data releaseUS retail sales for September fall more than expected, and the 10-year yield falls about 6bp. Around 9:00, the New York Fed's Empire State manufacturing index is also weak.
  2. 9:33–9:45
    Flash rallyWith no new information, the 10-year yield falls about 16bp, touching about 1.86% (intraday low to verify). Prices of cash Treasuries and futures rise together.
  3. 9:45–10:00
    ReversalPrices retrace almost the entire move within about 15 minutes.
  4. Close
    Small net changeThe 10-year yield ends the day at about 2.13%, a few basis points below its previous close, after an intraday range of about 37bp. (close to verify)

The Joint Staff Report estimated that a move of that size in so short a window, without major news, was many standard deviations from normal daily variation. Moves of this kind had previously been seen only on days of major policy announcements. (standard-deviation comparison to verify)

Mechanism: high volume, thin depth

Liquidity has several dimensions. On 15 October they moved in opposite directions. Trading volume reached record levels in both cash and futures markets, while the amount available to trade at the best prices fell sharply. Liquidity providers cut the size of their quotes or moved them away from the market, so each trade moved prices further.

  1. Weak data and crowded short positions create buying pressure.
  2. Liquidity providers, uncertain about the move, cut their quoted size or step away.
  3. Thinner order books mean each trade moves prices more.
  4. Fast price moves trigger stop-losses on short positions and further automated buying, and liquidity providers pull back further.

The review also found that "self-trading", where a single firm traded with itself, accounted for a notable share of volume in some periods, which complicated the reading of activity data. It found no evidence that any single firm or algorithm had caused the move, or of manipulation. (to verify)

Who was trading
  • Principal trading firms. Automated firms accounted for most trading on the main interdealer platforms and in futures during the event window. Overall, they continued to trade, but they reduced the depth they offered at the best prices. (shares to verify)
  • Bank dealers. Bank dealers widened their bid-ask spreads and reduced their share of liquidity provision during the most intense minutes, consistent with their limited capacity to warehouse risk.
  • Data gaps. Officials had no consolidated record of transactions in cash Treasuries. Reconstructing the event required collecting data from trading platforms and firms, which took months.
Policy response
  • Joint Staff Report (July 2015). The Treasury, the Fed, the New York Fed, the SEC and the CFTC jointly analysed the event and the changes in market structure behind it.
  • Request for information (January 2016). The Treasury consulted publicly on the structure of the Treasury market.
  • Transaction reporting. From July 2017, FINRA member firms had to report secondary-market transactions in Treasuries to TRACE, giving officials the transaction data they had lacked in 2014.
  • Longer-term reforms. The episode fed into later work on the market's resilience, including the SEC's 2023 central-clearing mandate for Treasury cash and repo trades and its 2024 rule requiring large liquidity providers to register as dealers. (verify status of the 2024 dealer rule)
Financial-stability reading
  • Volume is not depth. A market can trade record volumes while the amount available at any given price is very small. Measures of liquidity that rely on volume can give false comfort.
  • New liquidity providers behave differently. Automated firms provide tight prices in normal conditions but carry little inventory and can withdraw within seconds. The shift from dealers to PTFs changed how liquidity behaves under stress.
  • Safe assets can move violently. The deepest sovereign market in the world produced a move that would have been unusual for an equity index. The Bund tantrum showed the same pattern six months later in the euro area.
  • Authorities need data. Supervisors could not see the market they were responsible for monitoring. Data collection became a core part of the reform agenda.
  • A precursor, not a crisis. Prices recovered within minutes and no firm failed. The same questions about intermediation capacity returned at much larger scale in the 2019 repo spike, March 2020 and the April 2025 Treasury sell-off.
